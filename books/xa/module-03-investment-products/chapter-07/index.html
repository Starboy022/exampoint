7.1

Types of investment

There are many investment opportunities. At the broadest level, investments split into non-financial and financial. Non-financial investments are physical assets — real estate, gold, commodities. Financial investments are an exchange of cash flows for a period of time: financial instruments are essentially claims on future cash flows.

Financial instruments can be sliced three ways:

  • By claim on cash flows → debt vs equity (the two generic types).
  • By market where they trade → public vs private markets.
  • By maturity profile → capital market (long-term) vs money market (short-term).
The same financial instrument can be classified three different ways at once — by its claim (debt/equity), by its market (public/private) and by its maturity (capital/money market).

7.2

Equity

Equity shares represent ownership in a company, giving holders profit-sharing and voting rights. Shareholders are residual claimants — they receive profits only after all other obligations are met. Returns come through dividends and capital appreciation. Over the long term, equities provide time diversification benefits, smoothing risk fluctuations and improving risk-adjusted returns.

Preference shares — the hybrid security

In addition to equity shares, companies may issue preference shares. They rank above equity shares for dividends and for the distribution of net assets on liquidation, but generally carry no voting rights (unless stated otherwise). Because they mix a fixed dividend (debt-like) with equity features (they can be perpetual), they are called hybrid or blended securities. Preference shares come in combinations:

DVRs in India — Tata Motors, 2008

Governance normally follows 'one share, one vote'. Shares with Differential Voting Rights (DVRs) may carry superior or inferior voting rights, or differential dividends. Common in the West, DVRs have seen limited adoption in India despite being permitted since 2000. Tata Motors was among the first to issue DVRs in 2008 — 1/10th the voting rights but a 5% higher dividend. Pantaloons Retail, Gujarat NRE Coke and Jain Irrigation Systems also issued DVRs.

7.3

Fixed income

Debt instruments (also called fixed income instruments) are contracts containing a promise to pay a stream of cash flows over the term of the contract. A debt contract can be transferable (sellable to another investor) or non-transferable. It sets the borrower's financial requirements and restrictions, and the holder's rights if the borrower defaults. Debt is issued by companies, municipalities, states and sovereign governments. By issuer, it splits into government and corporate debt — and government securities are the largest component of the debt market in India and worldwide.

Gilt-edged and the credit spread

A G-Sec is a tradeable instrument issued by the Central or State Government. Short-term ones (maturity < 1 year) are treasury bills; long-term ones (> 1 year) are bonds / dated securities. The Central Government issues both; States issue only bonds — the State Government Securities (SGSs). G-Secs carry practically no default risk, hence risk-free gilt-edged. Corporate paper pays higher rates to compensate for default risk; the yield gap over a same-maturity G-Sec is the credit spread — a risk premium that widens with the probability of default.

7.4

Commodities

Commodities split into soft (grown — corn, wheat, soybean, sugar) and hard (mined — gold, silver, oil, copper, aluminium). Soft commodities are perishable, so they show high price volatility and higher business-cycle risk (prices track the demand and supply of the end products); weather prediction matters. Both types have historically shown low correlation with stocks and bonds, so they add diversification to a portfolio. Gold — a reserve asset for centuries — has become the safe-haven asset thanks to its global acceptability, attractive in times of uncertainty and geopolitical crisis. Crucially, commodities generate no current income: the investor counts only on capital appreciation.

Why low correlation matters

The benefit of diversification rests on correlation — a measure of how two investments move together. The lower the correlation between investments, the higher the diversification benefit (i.e. the greater the reduction in risk). Because soft commodities and gold move differently from equities and bonds, adding a slice of them can lower a portfolio's overall risk. Exposure to soft commodities is often taken through derivative contracts (forwards, futures) — so the risks must be understood.

7.5

Real estate

Real estate is a significant asset class and a driver of economic growth. It offers diversification, has historically been a good inflation hedge, and can be held for capital appreciation or regular rental income. It is usually a long-term investment, classified into commercial and residential (and further by tier I/II/III cities). Direct property involves large commitments — so real estate funds and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) let investors take exposure with smaller outflows.

7.6

Structured products

Structured products are customized, sophisticated investments giving risk-adjusted exposure to traditional assets or to assets otherwise hard to obtain. They use derivatives heavily to build the desired risk exposure, often linked to equity indices, sector indices, a themed basket of stocks, currencies, interest rates or commodities. Terms can be customized (short or long), they need larger denominations, and may offer protection from 0% to 100% and/or attractive yields. But performance depends on the underlying strategy and market conditions — so they must not be treated as capital-protection or guaranteed/assured-return products. Their most common form is the Market Linked Debenture (MLD).

7.7

Distressed securities

Distressed securities are the equity and debt of companies in financial distress or near bankruptcy. They may be available at huge discounts, but valuing them needs higher skill and greater experience than regular securities. Also called 'fallen angels', many funds and institutional investors are prohibited from holding them because of the high risk. They are a popular segment among hedge fund managers (with deep valuation and credit-analysis skills), and PMS and AIF products exist on distressed securities. Only evolved investors with the appetite and understanding should consider them, for risk diversification.

7.8

Other investment opportunities

Art, paintings and rare collectibles are emerging as long-term investments, generating moderate long-term returns with low correlation to equities and bonds (a diversification benefit). But they are big-ticket, each work is unique (not a standard product), the market is unregulated and illiquid, they generate no income (only capital appreciation), and demand specialized knowledge because of high information asymmetry and adverse selection. Art and painting funds exist for indirect exposure.

Investors can also invest abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) — up to USD 250,000 per year — for geographical and currency diversification and new choices.

LRS — USD 250,000 a year

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme lets a resident individual invest up to USD 250,000 abroad every year. It opens up geographical and currency diversification and several new choices for investors.

7.9

Channels for making investments

Investors can access any of these opportunities in one of two ways: directly, or through an intermediary offering managed-portfolio solutions.

The role of the RIA

Investors can take advice from a SEBI Registered Investment Adviser (RIA). Under the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013, only qualified professionals licensed by SEBI as RIAs can act as 'advisers'. After this regulation, mutual fund distributors, share brokers and insurance agents — who earlier acted as advisers — can no longer claim the title. RIAs are paid fees by the investors who hire them and are accountable only to those investors. They follow a strict code of conduct, advise in the investor's best interest, and must disclose conflicts of interest. They do risk profiling, assess needs and financial health, build financial plans, and instil discipline — helping investors build an optimum portfolio and make rational decisions.
