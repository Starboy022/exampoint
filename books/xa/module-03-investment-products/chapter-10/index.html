10.1

Basics of derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from the value of some other asset — the underlying. Underlyings span metals (gold, silver), energy (oil, coal, gas), agri commodities (wheat, coffee) and financial assets (shares, bonds). The derivative itself has no independent value; it simply tracks something else.

The two legal definitions

Under the SC(R)A, 1956, a derivative includes a security derived from a debt instrument/share/loan and a contract deriving value from the prices (or index of prices) of underlying securities — and derivatives are securities, so their trading is governed by the SC(R)A. Separately, Section 45U(a) of the RBI Act, 1934 defines a derivative (settled at a future date) whose value derives from a change in interest rate, FX rate, credit rating/index or price of securities — covering interest-rate swaps, forward-rate agreements and currency options.

Futures
Standardised, exchange-traded · clearing house as counterparty · margins both sides · obligation on both

Cash vs forward: the goldsmith example

On 9 Mar 2018 gold's spot price is ₹30,425/10g — pay and take delivery today, and it's a cash-market deal at the spot price. Agree instead to pay ₹30,450 and take delivery after 1 month, with nothing changing hands today, and it's a forward: you are long forward, the goldsmith is short forward, and both are obliged to honour it whatever gold does. Futures then take this forward idea and standardise it onto an exchange, with a clearing house guaranteeing settlement.

Why forwards fall short

💧 Liquidity risk

  • Tailor-made terms → other participants uninterested
  • Not listed or traded on exchanges
  • Hard to access or exit

🤝 Counterparty (default) risk

  • A party may default if it has an incentive to
  • Rice at ₹20/kg: if the market hits ₹30, the seller may walk; at ₹15, the buyer may walk
  • Also called default or credit risk

Plus a lack of transparency and settlement complications — all solved by moving contracts onto a centralised platform, which is exactly what futures do.

Options in depth: rights, moneyness & payoffs

An option gives its buyer (holder / long) the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying at a stated strike price, for a premium. The writer (seller / short) has the obligation but no right. A Call is a right to buy; a Put is a right to sell.

Structure of derivative markets

A derivative market forms when players wanting to manage price risk come together. In India, derivative products are available on indices, stocks, interest rates and commodities — plus OTC forward markets for agri commodities and OTC swap markets for interest rates. Two structures exist:

🤝 OTC markets

  • Terms mutually agreed between counterparties
  • Non-standard; depend on counterparty trust
  • Prevalent among institutions comfortable with each other
  • Forwards are OTC derivatives

🏛️ Exchange-traded markets

  • Standard contracts defined by an exchange
  • Settled through a clearing house
  • Buyers/sellers post margins → anonymous parties can trade
  • Futures are exchange-traded derivatives

Purpose of derivatives

Derivatives are a risk-management tool, used for three purposes — tap each:

The law of one price

Two identical assets can't trade at different prices in two markets — arbitrageurs buy in the cheaper market and sell in the costlier one, and their very trades close the gap (demand lifts the cheap market, supply pushes down the dear one). Prices can differ only to the extent of transaction costs — transport, storage, insurance, interest and the like.

Benefits, costs and risks

✅ Benefits

  • Enable hedging & better risk management (symmetrical + asymmetrical payoffs)
  • Enhance liquidity of underlying markets; reduce trading costs
  • Increase participation, information dissemination & price discovery

⚠️ Risks (leveraged instruments)

  • Counterparty (default), price (adverse move)
  • Liquidity (can't exit), legal/regulatory (enforceability)
  • Operational (fraud, poor documentation/execution)

Derivatives may be unsuitable for those with limited resources, experience or low risk tolerance. Participants should read the Model Risk Disclosure Document (issued by exchange members) before trading the cash or F&O segments.

Equity, currency & commodity derivatives

The basic concept is identical across underlyings — only the underlying changes. When it's a commodity (oil, wheat) it's a commodity derivative; when it's an exchange rate it's a currency derivative. Both futures and options exist on each.

🌾 Commodity derivatives

  • Standardised futures: quantity, quality, delivery date & place, permissible price moves
  • Enable farmers/traders/processors to hedge price volatility
  • Four categories: Bullion, Metals, Energy, Agriculture
  • Exchanges: MCX, NCDEX, ICEX, NSE, BSE (SEBI-registered)

💱 Currency derivatives

  • Every FX trade is a currency pair — buy one, sell another
  • Underlying = an exchange rate
  • Pairs: USD, EUR, GBP, JPY (vs Rupee)
  • Cross-currency F&O: EUR-USD, GBP-USD, USD-JPY
The biscuit-maker's wheat hedge

A biscuit manufacturer needing 10 tonnes of wheat for future use can buy wheat futures on a commodity exchange instead of the spot market. The standardised contract assures delivery of the physical commodity at the originally contracted price on a specified future date — whatever the price prevailing then.

Derivative markets, products & strategies

Derivative trading was introduced in India in June 2000; India's equity derivative markets are now among the largest in the world. A futures contract is priced on the cost-of-carry principle: F = S + carry cost. The gap is the interest on the money for the period, so no arbitrage profit exists between spot and futures.

Live Futures price (cost of carry)
The workbook's example: a stock at ₹100 spot, with a ₹20 carry cost (interest) for the 20-day period, gives a futures price of ₹120 — so F = S + carry cost = 100 + 20. The gap is the basis. Carry cost is a market-driven rate, not a fixed risk-free rate.

₹120
Basis (F − S) = ₹20
On the settlement day, carry cost is zero and F = S — this is spot-future convergence.
Live Spot-future arbitrage (annualised)
The workbook's example (3 Mar 2017): stock XYZ is ₹3,984 in cash and ₹4,032 in futures (delivery 30 Mar, i.e. 27 days). Buy cash, sell futures, lock the ₹48 gap → annualised return = (48 ÷ 3,984) × (365 ÷ 27) = 16.29% p.a. Spot-future convergence closes both legs at the same price on settlement, so the profit is locked in.

Option payoffs: long vs short

📈 Long on option (buyer)

  • Has the right to exercise
  • Potential loss limited to the premium paid
  • Profit depends on the underlying's price at exercise/expiry

📉 Short on option (writer)

  • Has the obligation, no right
  • Max profit = the premium received
  • Potential loss is theoretically unlimited
  • India: mostly European options (exercised on expiry)
