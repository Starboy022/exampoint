9.1

The debt market and its need in financing

The debt market lets borrowers — governments and private firms — issue debt securities that investors buy in the primary (fresh issue) or secondary (liquidity & price discovery) market. It channels funds from savers to risk-takers, fuelling economic growth. Unlike equity, debt is relatively safer as it is often secured by physical assets — but on default, recovery can be a lengthy legal process, so an issuer's goodwill matters in honouring obligations.

🔵 Debt financing

  • Borrowing with fixed obligations (coupon + principal)
  • No ownership transfer
  • Often secured; ranks ahead of equity
  • Relatively safer, lower return

🟢 Equity financing

  • Grants ownership rights
  • No contractual repayment
  • Residual claim, ranks last
  • Higher risk, higher expected return

India's debt market has three key segments:

① Government securities — G-secs, T-bills, SDLs② PSU & bank bonds③ Private-sector bonds, debentures & commercial papers

A well-developed debt market needs strong legal frameworks and robust credit-risk assessment to attract investors.

9.2

The bond market ecosystem

Because bonds create fixed obligations, they are called fixed income securities. The issuer agrees to ① pay a fixed coupon periodically and ② repay the fixed face value (principal) at maturity. Most bonds pay semi-annually (some annually, quarterly or monthly), except zero-coupon bonds, which pay no interest. All G-Secs are normally coupon-bearing with semi-annual coupons and a tenor of 5–30 years.

Three intrinsic features define a bond — its coupon, maturity (tenor) and principal (par) value. On term to maturity: obligations of ≤1 year are money-market securities; those >1 year are capital-market securities — so a long-term bond becomes a money-market security as it nears maturity.

Price vs par: discount and premium

Par value ≠ market price unless the coupon equals the prevailing market rate. If the market rate is above the coupon, the bond sells at a discount; if the market rate is below the coupon, it sells at a premium. Unlike equity, a company can have many bond issues outstanding at once — but a maximum of 12 ISINs can mature in one financial year (the limit applies to new ISINs; existing ones are re-issued).

The risks of fixed income securities

Two broad groups dominate: market risk (from changing interest rates) and credit / default risk (from the borrower's creditworthiness). Government bonds are treated as risk-free (low return); PSUs/banks sit in between; pure corporates are riskier (higher return). Tap each risk:

HTM neutralises interest-rate risk

An investor who holds till maturity (HTM) escapes interest-rate risk: whatever the price swings in between, on maturity they receive exactly what was contracted — face value plus the final coupon. The catch: if you want to sell before maturity, a rise in rates means a capital loss.

9.4

Pricing of a bond

A bond is valued with the present-value model: discount every future cash flow (coupons + principal) at a single discount rate and add them up. Par is the face value — typically ₹100 for a G-bond, ₹10,000 for a corporate bond. The market quotes bonds as a percentage of face value (a bid of 106.35 = 106.35% of par). Prices above par are premium, below par are discount — and at maturity every price is pulled to par (100).

A plain-vanilla bond's cash flows: level coupons (C) each period, and a final period that repays the coupon plus the principal (C + M). The bond's price is the present value of this stream.

Clean price vs dirty price

Between coupon dates, the dirty price = clean price + accrued interest — also called the full price or invoice price, the amount the buyer actually pays the seller. The market trades on the clean price, and all yield/price formulas use it. The price–yield relationship is inverse and non-linear (convex): longer maturity → higher sensitivity; the lower the coupon → the greater the price sensitivity.

The price–yield curve slopes downward and is convex: as the yield rises, the bond's price falls — but not in a straight line.
Live Perpetual bond value
A perpetual bond pays coupons forever and never repays face value. Value = coupon ÷ required yield. The workbook's example: an 8% coupon on ₹100 face value, with a required yield of 6%, is worth (100 × 8%) ÷ 6% = ₹133.33.

Day-count conventions

The Indian bond market uses 30/360 (every month counted as 30 days, every year as 360). The money market uses actual/365 — so T-Bills, being money-market instruments, follow the money-market convention. In Excel's YIELD(), Indian G-secs use frequency = 2 and basis = 4 (30/360). The concept of duration

Bond prices move with interest rates — and duration measures how much. Macaulay Duration is the time (in years) to recover the initial investment in present-value terms — the bond's payback / break-even period. The greater the duration, the more volatile the return to rate changes.

Key properties:

  • Duration is always ≤ the bond's life to maturity.
  • Only a zero-coupon bond has a duration equal to its remaining maturity.
  • Duration does not increase exponentially with maturity — it stagnates after a point.
  • Modified duration = Macaulay duration ÷ (1 + periodic rate) → shows the % price change per rate change, and is generally lower than Macaulay duration.
  • Convexity measures the curvature of the price–yield relationship.

9.8

Introduction to the money market

The money market handles ultra-short to short-term funds — maturities from overnight to one year. A liquid money market is critical to financial stability and is the primary transmission channel of the central bank's monetary policy. (The 2008 Global Financial Crisis was triggered partly by the seizing up of international overnight money markets.) Participants include public/private/foreign/co-operative banks, FIs, insurers, mutual funds, primary dealers, NBFCs, corporates and pension funds.

9.9

Introduction to the government debt market

The G-Sec market is the most active segment of Indian fixed income. Beyond funding the government, it supplies the benchmark interest rates for pricing other products and is an indirect channel for monetary policy. Key players: banks (for SLR), Primary Dealers (market-makers giving firm two-way quotes), insurers, mutual funds, pension funds, FPIs — and the RBI itself. The PDO of RBI handles issue, coupon and principal, and RBI acts as depository, merchant banker and RTA.

Reading a G-Sec: "5.77% GS 2030"

Issued 3 Aug 2020, maturing 3 Aug 2030 — a 10-year fixed-rate bond paying 2.885% every six months (half of the 5.77% annual coupon) on ₹100 face value, on 3 Feb and 3 Aug each year. When a coupon date is a holiday it is paid the next working day; but redemption on a holiday is paid the previous working day.

Introduction to the corporate debt market

Corporate debt still has a low share of India's total debt issuance; demand is largely institutional, and the lack of trading platforms with a guaranteed central counterparty (CCP) hampers an active secondary market for individuals. Still, it has grown on the back of better transparency and infrastructure. Key roles in the ecosystem:

The Debenture Trustee's teeth

In India 'debenture' and 'corporate bond' are used interchangeably. A Debenture Trustee (DT) — registered under the SEBI (Debenture Trustees) Regulations, 1993 — is compulsory when maturity exceeds 18 months. On default, a DT can appoint nominee directors on the issuer's board and sell the secured property to redeem the debentures. A 'Retail Individual Investor' bids for ₹2 lakh or less.

Bank FDs & DICGC cover

Bank fixed deposits run from 7 days to 10 years. Each depositor is insured up to ₹5 lakh by the DICGC, covering all deposits and interest across a bank's branches. Senior citizens (over 60) usually get 0.25%–0.75% extra. The specified 5-year tax-saver FD qualifies for Section 80C (up to ₹1.5 lakh) but carries a 5-year lock-in.
