8.1

Equity as an investment

Seekers of capital issue two broad types of securities: equity (ownership) and debt (a lender's rights). Equity investors — shareholders — hold a residual claim: a claim on the company's net assets (assets after all liabilities are paid). Because they are owners, not lenders, the company is not contractually obligated to repay their money or to make periodic payments like interest.

Shareholders get voting rights and, with a sizable stake, a say in management. They look for capital appreciation and dividend income — but neither is assured: dividends depend on profitability, appreciation on market conditions. Since all residual benefits flow to equity, its expected return should exceed debt's.

🟢 Equity

  • Ownership + voting rights
  • Residual claim on net assets
  • No obligation to repay or pay periodic sums
  • Returns: dividends + capital appreciation (neither assured)
  • Higher risk, higher expected return

🔵 Debt

  • Lender's rights, no ownership
  • Contractual periodic interest + repayment
  • Ranks ahead of equity
  • Returns: lower but stable
  • Lower risk, lower return

8.2

Diversifying equity risk: cross-sectional vs time-series

Diversification tames the risk that is specific to individual companies and sectors. It works two ways:

↔️ Cross-sectional

  • Spread across many stocks and sectors at one point in time
  • Cancels out company- and sector-specific (idiosyncratic) shocks
  • Cannot remove market risk — that's common to all

↕️ Time-series (time)

  • Spread the holding across time — stay invested for the long term
  • Long horizons reduce the fluctuation of risk
  • Improves risk-adjusted returns for equities
Adding more stocks (cross-sectional diversification) drives out company- and sector-specific risk, but the curve flattens at the market-risk floor — the part that can't be diversified away, only hedged.

The six risks of equity investments

Only market risk hits every listed asset and can't be diversified away (only hedged); the next two are idiosyncratic and can be diversified. Tap each:

Impact cost = the price of illiquidity

Liquidity risk is measured by impact cost: the percentage price movement a given order size (say ₹1 lakh) causes, from the average of the best bid and offer in the order book. It's computed for both buy and sell sides. Thinly-traded stocks have high impact cost — one large trade moves the price a lot; a lower impact cost means a more liquid stock. Market risk, by contrast, is proxied by beta.

Overview of the equity market

Equity securities are ownership claims on a company's net assets. The market offers a range of risk-return-liquidity profiles across listed and unlisted space. Listed equity is relatively more liquid; listed companies must abide by listing norms, making the space more regulated with better disclosures. A thorough understanding of the market is needed to allocate optimally to this asset class.

8.5.2

Fundamental analysis & the EIC framework

Fundamental analysis estimates intrinsic value from the fundamentals that drive it — future earnings/cash flows, interest rates and risk variables. Buy if market price is below intrinsic value (net of transaction cost); don't buy, or sell, if above. Fundamentalists believe price eventually converges to intrinsic value. It proceeds through Economy → Industry → Company (EIC) analysis, in one of two directions:

⬇️ Top-down

  • Start at the macro economy
  • Pick attractive industries
  • Zero in on companies

⬆️ Bottom-up

  • Start at company-specific factors
  • Move up to the industry
  • Then the macro factors affecting it

8.5.3

Economy, the business cycle & the industry life cycle

The macro-economic forecast should estimate GDP, inflation, interest rates and unemployment. The stock market is a leading economic indicator — prices reflect expectations of future activity, so the market tends to turn before the economy does (though sentiment and flows can make it run ahead or lag). Interest-rate moves hit sectors unevenly: banks/financials are the most interest-sensitive; pharma is less affected.

Company analysis & SWOT

Company analysis usually starts with financial statement analysis (P&L, balance sheet, cash flow) and ratios — read alongside industry trends and historical averages. SWOT then maps the firm: Strengths/Weaknesses are internal (competitive advantages/disadvantages); Opportunities/Threats are external (a favourable tax change is an opportunity; stringent regulation, a big competitor or changing technology is a threat). Firms compete via a defensive strategy (deflect competitive forces) or an offensive one (use strengths to shape them).

A good company ≠ a good investment

The stock of a great company — superior management, strong sales and earnings growth — can trade far above its intrinsic value, making it a poor investment. Always compare value to price. Price is a Fact but Value is an Opinion.

Estimating intrinsic value: DCF & asset-based

Once economy, industry and company are analysed, the analyst estimates intrinsic value — an educated estimate, since inputs are uncertain, so valuation is art as well as science. Three popular approaches:

Why cash flows, not profits?

DCF needs three things: the stream of future cash flows, their timing, and the investor's expected rate of return (the discount rate). Profits look like the natural inflow — but profits are accounting estimates, not facts: accrual accounting allows valid adjustments with no cash moving. So business profits and cash returns aren't directly comparable, which is why analysts estimate cash flows from profit figures.

Relative valuation & the ratio toolkit

Relative valuation standardises a company's financials into multiples (of earnings, cash flow, book value or sales) and compares them with similar companies, the industry or the market. The most common is the P/E ratio.

8.6

Combining relative valuation and DCF

DCF estimates intrinsic value directly, driven by the growth rate of cash flows and the discount rate. Relative multiples compare the price to value-relevant variables (earnings, cash flow, book value, sales). A deeper look shows multiples are a simplified version of DCF — both embed the same fundamental drivers of value. The P/E, for instance, rests on the same idea that an asset's value is the present value of its future returns.

8.7

Technical analysis

Technical analysis assumes all price-affecting information is already in the price — so technicians (chartists) ignore fundamentals and instead study patterns in historical price and volume to forecast direction and magnitude. Three elements matter: past prices (trend & direction), the volume behind moves (strength of the trend), and the time span observed.

Assumptions

Price = interaction of supply & demandSupply/demand driven by rational + irrational factorsAdjustments aren't instantaneous — prices move in trendsTrends persist for appreciable periodsTrends change as supply/demand shiftsShifts are detectable in market action

Technical vs fundamental

📊 Fundamental

  • Computes intrinsic value, compares to price
  • Believes price converges to value over time
  • For long-term investing
  • Needs extensive data collection

📈 Technical

  • Ignores intrinsic value; studies price/volume history
  • Forecasts future price moves & timing
  • For short-term trading
  • Not heavily dependent on accounting statements — saves data-gathering time

The saying: "identify a stock through fundamental analysis and do the timing through technical analysis." Even so, investors are encouraged to be long-term investors, not traders.

Rules & indicators

Popular tools include trend-line analysis, moving averages and Bollinger-Band analysis. In trend-line analysis, a technician trades the channel — buy near the trough (support) of a rising channel, sell near the peak.

A rising trend channel: the technician ideally buys near the troughs (support) and sells near the peaks (resistance).
