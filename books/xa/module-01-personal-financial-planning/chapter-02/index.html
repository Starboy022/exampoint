2.1

The core idea — money has a time value

The value of money does not stay the same at every point in time. A rupee available today is worth more than the same rupee in the future, because today's rupee can be invested to earn a return. Given a choice — with no uncertainty — every investor prefers cash now:

  • An instinctive preference for current consumption over future consumption, and
  • The ability to invest today's money (say, in a bank deposit) so it grows to more than the same amount received later.
₹100 today, placed in a 1-month deposit yielding 6% p.a., grows to ₹100.50. So ₹100 now ≡ ₹100.50 in a month — the extra ₹0.50 is the compensation for waiting.

The value attached to the same sum of money received at different points on the timeline is the time value of money (TVM). Money received earlier is always worth more than the same amount received later. Since almost every decision in finance involves cash flows spread over more than one period, TVM is a foundational principle.

Did you know? — TVM is 7,000 years old

Records from as far back as 5000 BC show the Mesopotamians, Hittites, Phoenicians and Egyptians charging interest on loans of olives, dates, seeds and animals. The idea was easiest to see with seeds: a planted seed yields a plant that produces more seeds — so a seed today was worth more than a seed next season.

Four things to keep straight

  • Discounting: future inflows are brought down to today's value using a discount rate (a.k.a. return rate / interest rate).
  • Compounding: present inflows are grown to a future value using a compound interest rate.
  • The further in the future a cash flow arrives, the lower its value today. ₹100 in a month > ₹100 in a year > ₹100 in five years.
  • The higher the discount rate, the lower the present value of a future cash flow.

For example, ₹1,000 received after 3 years is worth ₹864 today when discounted at 5%, but only ₹794 at 8%. Drag the rate below and watch it happen:

The four parameters in any TVM problem

Every time-value problem is really about solving for one of these four inputs, given the other three:

💵

Cash flows

A single amount, or a stream — even (an annuity) or uneven — flowing in or out.

📈

Rate of interest

Also called the compounding, discount or reinvestment rate.

🗓️

Time period

Annual, or a fraction of it — monthly, quarterly, half-yearly.

🔁

Frequency

How often the cash flows occur — and it may or may not be fixed.

2.2

Present Value — what a future sum is worth today

Present value (PV) is the amount you would pay today for a cash flow that arrives in the future. It brings a future value back to today's price by discounting it at the rate of return. For a single future receipt:

PV = C / (1 + r)n
  • C = the future cash flow (or FV)  ·  r = rate of return per period  ·  n = number of periods

When the cash flow repeats every period (a regular receipt), the present value of the whole stream is:

PV = C × [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ] / r
Worked example — Shyam's ₹6,500 a year

Shyam will receive ₹6,500 every year for the next 8 years, and the interest rate is 7%. Should he take the yearly stream, or a lump sum now — and what is that stream worth today?

Discounting each year's ₹6,500 back and adding them up (or using the annuity formula / Excel PV(0.07, 8, -6500)) gives a present value of ₹38,813.44. If someone offered him more than ₹38,813 as a lump sum today, he should take the lump sum.

2.3

Future Value & the power of compounding

Future value (FV) is what an amount invested today will be worth at some point in the future, growing at the compound rate:

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

The rate per period must match the frequency of compounding. If an investment pays 8% p.a. compounded quarterly, each period's rate is 8% ÷ 4 = 2%, and there are 4 periods a year. The more often interest compounds, the more interest earns interest — and the greater the return.

Compound growth is not a straight line — it curves upward, because each period's interest is added to the base that earns the next period's interest.
Worked example — Krishna's ₹5 lakh, three ways

Krishna invests ₹5,00,000 in a 5-year bank deposit at 8%. How much interest does he earn under three scenarios? Scroll to animate the comparison:

2.4

Rate of return — CAGR

Often you know how much an investment grew but need to express it as an annual rate, so you can compare it with other options. The Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) is that standard measure:

CAGR = (FV / PV)1/n − 1
  • In Excel: = RATE(n, , −PV, FV). e.g. ₹100 → ₹120 over 2 years = 9.54%.

CAGR is the accepted standard for return in financial markets — except for periods shorter than a year. It even works for fractional periods: convert days to years (÷365) in the exponent.

Two mutual-fund examples

Whole years: ₹10.50 invested, redeemed at ₹12.25 after 3 years → CAGR = (12.25/10.50)1/3 − 1 = 5.27%.

Fractional period: units bought at NAV ₹11, redeemed at ₹13.50 after 450 days (non-leap year) → CAGR = (13.50/11)365/450 − 1 = 18.07%.

2.5

Periodic payments — EMI (PMT) & loan tenure (NPER)

Many financial commitments are a regular payment — most commonly a loan's Equated Monthly Instalment (EMI). Excel's PMT function computes it from three inputs: the rate per period, the number of periods, and the loan value. The catch: for a monthly loan you must divide the annual rate by 12 and count the tenure in months.

Worked example — Satish's home loan

Satish wants a ₹30 lakh loan for 20 years at 6.5% (monthly reset). Rate per period = 6.5%/12; periods = 240. PMT(0.065/12, 240, −3000000) = ₹22,367 a month.

If the rate falls to 6.25%, the EMI drops to ₹21,928 — a ₹439 saving from a 0.25% cut. Slide the rate below to see it move.

How long to clear a loan? — NPER

Flip the question: given a loan amount, a fixed monthly payment and the rate, NPER tells you how many months clearing it will take. For a ₹5,00,000 loan at 8% with a ₹12,000 EMI: NPER(0.08/12, −12000, 500000) = 48.97 months. This lets an adviser match the tenure to an EMI the client can actually afford.

2.6

Annuities — a stream of regular payments

An annuity is a sum of money paid at regular intervals — monthly, quarterly or annually. A pension is the classic example. Annuities come in two flavours by how the return behaves, and two types by when the payment lands.

Ordinary annuity vs. annuity due — timing changes value

The single difference is when in the period the payment is made:

  • Ordinary annuity — payment at the end of each period (most annuities, e.g. a bond coupon). Excel: leave type as 0.
  • Annuity due — payment at the start of each period. Excel: set type = 1.

Receiving each payment one period earlier means it can be invested sooner — so an annuity due is always worth more. Take ₹5,000 a year for 4 years at 10%:

Ordinary annuity — paid at year-end  PV(0.1,4,-5000)₹0
Annuity due — paid at year-start  PV(0.1,4,-5000,,1)₹0
Same ₹5,000 × 4 years at 10% — the annuity due is worth ₹1,585 more purely because each payment arrives a year earlier. (Annuity due PV = ordinary PV × (1 + r).)
Annuity tables

Instead of the full formula you can use an annuity factor from a table. To value ₹12,000 a year for 10 years at 5%: the factor is 7.7217, so PV = 12,000 × 7.7217 = ₹92,660 — exactly what PV(0.05, 10, −12000) returns.

2.7

Perpetuity — a cash flow that never ends

A perpetuity is a stream of equal cash flows that continues forever — there is no finite end date. Real-world examples are perpetual bonds (they pay a coupon year after year with no maturity) and certain lifelong pensions. Summing the infinite discounted series collapses to a strikingly simple formula:

PV = C / r
  • C = the constant periodic cash flow  ·  r = the discount rate
Worked example — a perpetual bond

A perpetual bond pays ₹10,000 of interest every year, forever. At a discount rate of 8%: PV = 10,000 ÷ 0.08 = ₹1,25,000. That is the most a rational investor would pay for the never-ending ₹10,000 stream.
