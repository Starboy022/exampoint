3.1

Importance of cash flow management in personal finance

Personal finance is about looking at the sources of income and expenses of a household and ensuring there is a surplus — savings — that can be allocated to investments to meet future goals. The starting point of the whole process is cash flow: the time and amount of income earned, set against the time and amount spent as expense.

Timing matters as much as amount. A salaried individual receives a monthly income and has monthly expenses like electricity, telephone and household staff wages — income and expense are naturally synchronised. But even a slight mismatch — income arriving at irregular intervals against evenly spread expenses — forces a household into debt, which comes at the cost of interest, eating further into savings.

Sunita earns ₹50,000 a month, but ₹15,000 of that is reimbursements she must claim separately. In a month she forgets to claim them, the actual cash in hand is only ₹35,000 — enough to throw a budget built on ₹50,000 into a cash crunch.

Cash flow mismanagement is not just a spreadsheet problem — sustained tension over money disrupts a household's peace and, over time, can affect health. Good cash flow management gives a sense of control and empowerment: funds are available as and when required, and the household runs smoothly.

Why cash flow comes first

Cash flow management is the starting point of the entire personal finance process. Every later step — budgeting, saving, investing, insuring, retiring — assumes that income and expenses are already under control.

3.2

Preparing a household budget

An investment adviser plays an important role in helping a household understand its budget. Preparing one starts with listing every head of income — some regular (salary), some irregular (investment income) — and every head of expense.

Expenses fall into three buckets:

Total expenses subtracted from total income gives the household's savings. There is also a subtler distinction: the monthly surplus in hand is calculated after mandatory deductions such as PF and NPS contributions — these are then added back to arrive at the actual savings, since they still belong to the individual.

Worked example — the ABC family's monthly budget

Mr. A earns ₹65,000 (₹50,000 salary + ₹15,000 investment income) and Ms. B earns ₹72,000 (₹60,000 salary + ₹12,000 investment income) — total income ₹1,37,000. Mandatory expenses (PF ₹9,000 + tax ₹12,000 + loan repayment ₹12,000 = ₹33,000), essential living expenses (₹64,000) and discretionary expenses (₹15,000) add up to total expenses of ₹1,12,000. ₹18,000 goes into investments. That leaves a monthly surplus in hand of ₹7,000 — and once the PF contribution is added back, total savings of ₹34,000, a savings ratio of 24.8%.

3.3

Cash inflows and outflows

3.3.1 Cash management

A budget can show a healthy surplus on paper, yet the household can still face a cash crunch — the income and expense figures must be backed by actual cash. Sunita's case (above) shows the inflow side of this problem: components of salary like reimbursements may not be claimed every month, so actual cash received can fall short of the income figure used in the budget.

The expense side has an equivalent risk: if too many expenses cluster at the start of the month, the cash balance can run low for the rest of the month, leaving little room for anything unplanned. The practical fix is to always keep a surplus buffer in the bank, or set aside a small separate fund purely to smooth short-term cash mismatches — distinct from a full emergency fund, which exists to handle a loss of income, not a timing hiccup.

3.3.2 Income and expenditure statement

An income and expenditure statement records the actual income earned and actual expenses incurred over a specific period — a month or a year — so the real figures can be compared against the budget. It differs subtly from a pure cash statement: an item is recorded in the period it pertains to, even if the cash moves later.

💼

Salary received next month

Income is booked in the earlier period it relates to, even though the amount lands in the bank a day or two into the next month.

💳

Credit card spend

The expense is booked in the period the purchase was made — not the later period when the credit-card bill is actually paid.

The statement shows the extent of surplus or savings generated and highlights exactly where the money is going — an essential input for the next step, effective control through budgeting.

3.4

Budgeting and forecasting

Income must be adequate to meet current expenses and build the assets that fund future needs. Prudent financial management requires a defined, targeted level of savings — meeting goals cannot be left to whatever happens to be left over. A budget is what lets a household plan income and expenses so the available money is used in the best possible way.

Steps to making a budget

  • 1. List and total the regular, definite incomes receivable in the period.
  • 2. List and deduct mandatory expenses from total income — what's left is disposable income.
  • 3. Identify essential living expenses and deduct them from disposable income.
  • 4. List discretionary expenses and deduct them to arrive at savings.

If savings look inadequate once this is done, income usually cannot be expanded much further — the focus has to shift to managing expenses. High mandatory expenses often trace back to loan-repayment pressure, where a debt-rationalisation exercise can help. Discretionary and, to some extent, living expenses are where a household can cut back or postpone spending without compromising required savings.

Forecasting — six things to keep in mind

Forecasting is predicting how the future will play out, on a specific basis, taking evolving conditions into account. It rests on assumptions that can change, often benefits from expert input since no one person sees every factor, and improves with the forecaster's experience. The forecast then feeds the budget — and is checked against what actually happens so deviations can be corrected.

3.5

Monitoring budgets and provision for savings

Preparing a budget is not the end of the process — monitoring it is just as vital. Monitoring means recording the actual income and expenses that arose and comparing them to the plan. It is unlikely every line item will match exactly; the size of the variance is itself a signal of how robust the original budgeting was.

Where an item consistently misses its target, the planning is off track and needs correcting going forward — either raise the budgeted figure, or change behaviour to bring the actual figure back in line.

Worked example — the conveyance overshoot

A household budgets ₹4,000 a month for conveyance, but the actual average comes to ₹6,000 for several months running. The consistent miss signals that either the budget figure needs raising, or the mode of conveyance needs to change to bring cost back down.

The ultimate objective of budgeting is a genuine surplus — income exceeding expenses — because every further step in financial planning depends on savings being generated. If the gap between actual and desired savings is small, closing it is easy. A large gap needs a step-by-step approach:

  • Set an achievable target rise in the savings rate, e.g. 10%.
  • Once achieved, set the next target — a constant, ongoing effort.
  • Eliminate wasteful spending and cut discretionary expenses.
  • Divert lump-sum or windfall receipts directly into savings.
  • Do not commit a higher income to new spending — leave it free to raise the savings rate.

3.6

Creating a personal balance sheet and net worth

A personal balance sheet brings together every asset and liability at a single point in time. Assets include physical assets (property, car) and financial assets (equity, debt, mutual funds), plus cash, bank balances and receivables. Liabilities include every loan and outstanding payment, including short-term debt like credit card dues. The difference between the two is the net worth of the individual or household — and a higher net worth always indicates a stronger financial position.

🏠

Physical assets

Real estate, gold, precious metals. Natural inflation hedge, but illiquid, lightly regulated, and need specific skills to buy, manage and sell.

📄

Financial assets

Bank deposits, equity, debt, mutual funds. Standardised, regulated, often more liquid, and can be invested in small units.

Loans taken to buy appreciating assets add to long-term wealth; loans taken to buy financial assets (leveraging) are riskier because of the higher volatility in such asset prices. Assets acquired purely from savings, without any liability, add the most to a household's financial strength.

Worked example — Lakshmi's personal balance sheet

Lakshmi owns a house (₹18,12,000) and a car (₹3,25,000), and holds mutual funds (₹8,40,000), PPF (₹3,20,000), shares (₹2,80,000) and cash & bank (₹45,000) — total assets ₹36,22,000. Against this she has a housing loan of ₹12,02,000 and credit card dues of ₹5,000 — total liabilities ₹12,07,000. Her net worth is ₹36,22,000 − ₹12,07,000 = ₹24,15,000.

3.7

Creating a budget and savings plan

Building a budget and savings plan step by step ensures nothing important is missed:

  • a. Gather all details of income and expense sources first — the budget is only as good as this base information.
  • b. Double-check that no head of income or expense has been missed — wider coverage makes a better budget.
  • c. Base figures on actual past expenses, or the likely change if circumstances have shifted.
  • d. Adjust figures for the time period — e.g. a seasonal spike in vegetable prices, or a month with family birthdays needing more discretionary allocation.
  • e. Build in a contingency figure so the budget stays robust when something unexpected happens.
  • f. Set the required savings figure based on goals, and achieve it by curtailing expenses within limits.
  • g. Allocate the savings amount before it can be spent elsewhere — a direct, automatic transfer to investments each month works best.

Making the savings transfer a fixed line item in the budget — rather than "whatever is left over" — is what ensures the goal is actually met.

3.8

Contingency planning

Investment advisers must prepare clients for the contingencies that can derail an otherwise sound financial plan:

The emergency fund

An emergency fund should be the first goal a household saves towards — protection against a loss or reduction of income. It should cover roughly six months of expenses and be held in liquid assets for easy access. If income security is high, it can be held in a laddered way: around three months' worth in fully liquid assets, and the rest in less-liquid assets that earn a better return. If used, it should be replenished as soon as possible, and its adequacy reviewed annually or whenever a large new expense (such as an EMI) is added.

Sizing the fund

If regular monthly expenses are ₹79,000 and loan servicing adds ₹12,000, total monthly outgo is ₹91,000. Covering six months of that outgo means an emergency fund target of ₹91,000 × 6 = ₹5,46,000.

3.9

Evaluating the financial position of clients — personal finance ratios

Just as analysts use ratios to assess companies, investment advisers use personal finance ratios to give a numerical snapshot of a client's financial position — calculated from income, expenses, savings and investment data, and reviewed periodically (typically annually) to track trends and flag areas needing action.

3.9.1 Savings ratio & expense ratio

Savings Ratio = Savings per year / Annual Income

Savings is not limited to a savings-account balance — it includes money invested in FDs, mutual funds, shares, debentures, PPF, NSCs, post-office deposits, real estate, gold and more, plus amounts like an employer's PF contribution that accrue to the individual without passing through their hands immediately.

Worked example — XYZ's payslip

XYZ's gross salary is ₹10,000 (including the employer's ₹500 PF contribution); net take-home is ₹9,000 after the employee's own ₹500 PF deduction. Interest income is ₹200; monthly expenses are ₹7,000. Total income = ₹10,000 + ₹200 = ₹10,200. Savings = (₹9,000 + ₹200 + ₹500 + ₹500) − ₹7,000 = ₹3,200. Savings ratio = ₹3,200 ÷ ₹10,200 = 31.4%.

3.9.2 & 3.9.3 Total Assets and Total Liabilities

Total assets are the current value of everything the individual has accumulated — shares, debentures, mutual funds, real estate, gold, PF, superannuation and more. A conservative approach excludes personal jewellery, the family's self-occupied residence, and other personal-use assets like a car when estimating assets actually available to meet financial goals.

Total liabilities are every loan and form of credit taken, whether from institutions (banks, NBFCs) or personal sources (friends, relatives) — long-term and secured (a mortgage) or short-term and unsecured (credit card dues, personal loans, which tend to be more expensive). Greater liability means greater future repayment pressure on income.

3.9.4 – 3.9.6 Leverage, Net Worth & Solvency Ratio

Leverage Ratio = Total Liabilities / Total Assets
Solvency Ratio = Net Worth / Total Assets = 1 − Leverage Ratio
Worked example — an investor's leverage

Real estate ₹50L + investments & bank balances ₹10L + PF ₹5L = total assets ₹65L. Outstanding home loan ₹10L + credit card dues ₹2L + a friend's loan ₹1L = total liabilities ₹13L. Leverage ratio = 13/65 = 20%. Net worth = ₹52L. Solvency ratio = 52/65 = 80%.

Reading the leverage ratio

Higher leverage means more risk. A ratio greater than 1 (100%) means assets are inadequate to cover liabilities — the individual is technically insolvent. Leverage is naturally high right after a large debt-funded purchase like real estate, and moderates over time as the asset appreciates. A critical benchmark: net worth should always be positive.

3.9.7 & 3.9.8 Liquid Assets and Liquidity Ratio

Liquid assets can be converted to cash quickly to meet expenses or emergencies — savings bank balances, fixed deposits maturing within 6 months, and liquid mutual funds. Assets like real estate or closed-end fund units take time to sell at fair value. Shares and open-end equity or debt schemes can technically be sold fast, but their value fluctuates too much in the short term to be treated as reliably liquid for this purpose.

Liquidity Ratio = Liquid Assets / Monthly Expenses

A ratio of at least 4 to 6 indicates comfort — the household could cover 4–6 months of expenses purely from liquid assets, even with a complete loss of income.

Worked example — NFG's liquidity

Monthly expenses (incl. loan repayments) ₹1.5 lakh. Of NFG's holdings, only the savings bank account (₹7L), short-term FDs (₹2L) and a liquid mutual fund (₹4L) count as liquid — ₹13L total. Equity shares, long-term FDs, other open-end schemes and closed-end fund units are excluded. Liquidity ratio = 13 ÷ 1.5 = 8.66 — a comfortable position.

3.9.9 Financial Assets Ratio

Financial Assets Ratio = Financial Assets / Total Assets × 100

Financial assets (shares, debentures, bank deposits, PPF, mutual funds) offer greater liquidity, flexibility and ease of small, frequent investing than physical assets (gold, precious metals, diamonds, real estate). A higher financial-assets ratio is preferred especially when goals are near and income or funds will soon be needed.

Worked example — PQR's asset mix

Shares ₹5L + FDs ₹10L + mutual funds ₹12L = financial assets ₹27L. Land ₹9L + gold ₹14L = physical assets ₹23L. Total ₹50L. Financial assets ratio = 27/50 × 100 = 54%.

3.9.10 Debt to Income Ratio

Debt to Income Ratio = Monthly Debt Servicing / Monthly Income

Unlike the leverage ratio (which measures debt used to acquire assets), this measures whether current income can comfortably service all debt obligations — principal and interest combined. Lenders use it to judge eligibility for additional loans.

Worked example

Monthly income ₹1.5 lakh; loan commitments ₹60,000 a month. Debt to income ratio = 60,000 ÷ 1,50,000 = 40% — right at the excessive threshold.
