4.1

The purpose or need of debt

Debt finances goals when self-funding is inadequate. Even if income exceeds expenses, few big-ticket assets can be bought outright — most people cannot buy a house without a loan; waiting until they had saved the full price could push the purchase to near-retirement. Lenders protect themselves by taking a charge, security, pledge or mortgage over the asset — a home loan secured this way is a mortgage.

Debt is not inherently bad — it must be seen in perspective, according to what it funds. Debt to acquire a house is not bad. Using borrowed funds instead of own funds — leverage — can even raise the return on an investment (the Modigliani–Miller idea).

Education loan vs. raiding retirement savings

To fund a child's education, an education loan can beat drawing down a retirement account. Future income repays the loan easily, while the existing corpus keeps compounding untouched. Preserving the compounding effect of existing investments is the whole point.

4.2

Debt in cash-flow management — the DTI ratio

Some debt is good, but how much depends on the household. The key gauge is the debt-to-income (DTI) ratio — the share of income going to debt servicing. There is no single "optimal" DTI, but lenders agree lower is better, and each sets its own ceiling for loan approval.

Worked example

An employee earns ₹15,000/month and pays ₹7,500 in debt servicing. DTI = 7,500 ÷ 15,000 = 50% — far too high. With half the income gone to debt, little is left for regular expenses, emergencies or investing. A low DTI also leaves room to borrow more in a crisis.

4.2.1 Consumption vs. investment expenditure

Consumption expenditure creates no income-generating asset once spent — a restaurant meal, clothes, a salon visit. Investment expenditure creates an asset that can generate future income — a bond, a property. Some outflows look like consumption but are really investment: a child's education, if it lifts the family's future earning power. The adviser's job is to make these trade-offs explicit and prioritise savings for goals ahead of discretionary spending.

Flip the equation

Change the habit from Income − Expenses = Savings (save whatever's left) to Income − Savings = Expenses (pay yourself first, then spend). An SIP auto-debited from salary enforces exactly this. Also useful: zero-base budgeting — build each month's budget from scratch, justifying every expense, instead of copying last month's.

Advisers also look for holes in the budget (untracked credit-card spends on eating out, needless gadget upgrades) and handle windfalls — an unexpected bonus, inheritance or lottery — by setting a healthy portion aside, often to pre-pay loans, rather than inflating lifestyle. A windfall can be parked in risk-free liquid assets until a deployment decision is reached, and should never be relied on to rescue a chronically bad financial situation.

4.3

Leverage and debt counselling

When advising on debt, four factors shape the plan. Tap each card.

The true cost of revolving credit

A credit card is interest-free only if you pay the full bill by the due date. Carry a balance over ("revolving credit") and the meter runs. At 3% per month, the compounded annual cost is (1 + 3%)12 − 1 = 42.6% — which is why revolving credit should be avoided. Unorganised lenders (pawnbrokers, moneylenders) can charge 5% a month, i.e. 60–80% a year. Slide the monthly rate to feel the compounding:

4.3.4 Debt re-scheduling

If repayments become unmanageable, act early. Options, roughly in order: rank debts by cost and kill the costliest (credit cards, personal loans) first; sell the funded asset and pre-pay (mind pre-payment charges); refinance a costly loan with a cheaper one (a credit-card balance transfer); extend the tenor to cut the monthly outgo (but total interest rises); or, as a last resort, ask the lender for a revised schedule. Note: formal re-scheduling hurts the credit score, affecting future loan access and pricing.

4.4

Credit bureaus and the credit score

A credit score is a number assigned by a credit information bureau from your credit behaviour and repayment history. Bureaus are licensed by the RBI and governed by the Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005. Lenders (banks, HFCs, NBFCs) report to them monthly, and use the resulting score and report to decide loan eligibility, interest rate and credit limits. The CIBIL TransUnion Score is a three-digit number from 300 to 900 — higher is better.

Illustrative CIBIL score (300–900). Build a strong score by paying every due on time, keeping credit utilisation low, holding a healthy mix of secured & unsecured credit, and making credit enquiries only when needed. Joint-holder / guarantor defaults also drag your score down.
RBI rule — one free report a year

Every individual is entitled to one free full credit report each year from each bureau — in India, CIBIL, Experian, CRIF Highmark and Equifax. It must match what lenders see, let you rectify errors (wrong info, a closed loan still showing open), and typically shows 2–3 years of repayment history over a Jan–Dec calendar year, accessible electronically after authentication (UID, PAN). Beware fake "check your score" sites phishing for personal data — use only genuine bureau tie-ups.

4.8

Types of borrowing

The common borrowing products, each suited to a different need and risk profile.

Ranking by cost

Credit-card debt is the most expensive way to borrow (and the easiest — one swipe). Loans against securities, gold or property are cheaper than a personal loan or card outstanding, so are preferred if you have an asset. P2P loans are unsecured, small and short-term with high rates and no recourse on default.

4.9

Loan & EMI calculations

The EMI is found with the PMT function on a reducing-balance basis. For monthly loans, divide the annual rate by 12 and count the tenor in months.

EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ [ (1+r)n − 1 ]
Worked example — sizing debt against income

A ₹1,00,000 loan at 12% for 5 years gives an EMI of ₹2,224.44/month. If the borrower earns ₹10,000/month and this is the only debt, DTI = 2,224.44 ÷ 10,000 = 22.24%. Extend the tenor to 6 years and the EMI falls to ₹1,955.02 → DTI 19.55%. Add another loan with a ₹1,000 EMI and total servicing is ₹2,955.02 → DTI 29.55%.

4.10

Loan restructuring — present value of future payments

When a borrower hits financial stress (job loss, pay cut), the lender restructures rather than let the loan turn into a non-performing asset. A restructuring changes the EMI, the tenor, the outstanding, or the rate. Two lenses matter: affordability for the client, and the present value of all future payments — because the EMI stream is exactly the cash flow whose PV equals the loan.

Worked example — a ₹3 lakh loan at 7%

A ₹3,00,000 loan over 3 years (36 months) at 7% has an EMI of ₹9,263 — PMT(0.07/12, 36, −300000). Restructuring options:

  • Extend to 5 years (60 months): EMI drops to ₹5,940 — lower outgo, but more total interest.
  • Lender takes a haircut to ₹2,00,000 over the same 3 years: EMI falls to ₹6,175 — real relief, lower present value repaid.

Unless the client checks the present value of the new cash flows, they can't tell whether a change is genuine relief or just re-timed pain.

Amortisation & repayment with varying rates

Every EMI is part interest and part principal — Excel's IPMT and PPMT split them. In the early years the interest slice is large and the principal slice small; the mix flips over the loan's life. The rate decides how lopsided the first EMI is.

4.12

Criteria to evaluate loans

Two loans with the same headline rate can cost very differently. Weigh all of these:

  • aInterest rate & method. A lower rate is better — but insist on reducing-balance calculation, not flat, so repayments actually cut the interest base.
  • bReset period. How often the rate is reset. A long reset (a year) delays the benefit of rate cuts — but also delays rate hikes.
  • cCharges. Processing fees, legal and other charges (a % of the loan or a fixed sum) add to cost above the interest. Waivers save money.
  • dTenor offered. A longer tenor cuts the EMI but raises total interest — crucial for those who need a low EMI.
  • eBenchmark. External benchmarks (repo rate) are transparent and transmit fast; internal bank benchmarks move slowly. Banks now favour floating-rate loans, since on a fixed-rate loan a rise in market rates squeezes their margin.
4.13

Rate change: adjust the EMI or the tenure?

Most long-term loans float, so the linked benchmark's moves change the rate — and the borrower must choose: keep the EMI and change the tenure, or keep the tenure and change the EMI.

Default preference

The EMI was chosen to be affordable given income and savings, so it's usually easier to hold the EMI and flex the tenure — you track one steady amount, and a rate rise won't suddenly strain the budget. But if a high-rate loan has fallen a lot (so tenure has already shrunk), a one-time EMI cut can reset a comfortable monthly outgo; and where EMI pressure is high, a rate fall can be taken as a lower EMI.

4.14

Invest the money, or pay off the loan?

Given a lump sum or windfall, should the client invest it or repay debt? It depends on the loan burden, the absolute and relative size of the amount, the loan rate vs the expected investment return, and the risk of the asset class. A high-cost credit-card or personal loan is almost always worth repaying first. But if a diversified investment can compound at, say, 12% while the loan costs 7%, investing can win over the long run — provided the client accepts the extra risk.
