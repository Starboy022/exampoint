1.1

Understand the concept of Financial Planning

Financial planning aims at ensuring that a household or individual has adequate income or resources to meet current and future expenses and needs. Regular income — from a profession, salary, business or investments — funds routine activities, but income must also provide for periods with no income (like retirement), unexpected expenses (a large medical bill), and future high-cost needs (a child's education, a home).

A portion of current income is therefore saved and channelled into assets that will meet these requirements. In short: financial planning streamlines income, expenses, assets and liabilities to take care of both current and future needs for funds.

Worked example — Vinod, 40

Vinod earns Rs. 2 lakh a month and saves about Rs. 40,000 after routine expenses, loan payments, tax-saving investments, deposits, bonds and mutual funds, plus life insurance premiums for himself and his wife. He's the sole earner and believes he's managing well. Would financial planning still help him?

Yes — a formal process would surface questions he hasn't structured an answer to: Does he have an emergency fund? Adequate life and health cover? A sized corpus for each future goal? Is he saving enough to reach it? How much investment risk can and should he take — and is that risk being managed?

1.2

Understand the need for financial planning

Investors face a huge range of financial products and services, but not every product suits every client — and most clients can't independently judge how to choose between them. Financial planning bridges this gap: the Investment Adviser understands both the mechanics of the products and the specific needs of the client.

1.3.1 Personal financial analysis — goal setting

The process starts with setting goals: a clear target with specific detail (not "I want to be rich" but "I want Rs. 50 lakh a year"), that is measurable, realistic given the client's savings capacity, and time-bound. Once listed, goals must be prioritised — important goals like children's education and retirement ahead of discretionary spending like a car.

1.3.2 Cash flow management & budgeting

Even a client who spends less than they earn can run into cash flow problems if inflows and outflows are mistimed — for example, a large expense early in the month before income arrives. This can force costly short-term borrowing. A budget — a list of inflows and outflows with their timing — is the standard tool to prevent this.

1.3.3 Insurance Planning

Insurance is a risk-transfer mechanism: a small premium buys protection against a large, unexpected loss. Life insurance covers temporary loss of income (disability) and permanent loss of income (death). Health and accident insurance address unexpected medical costs. General insurance covers loss or damage to property. Planning here means estimating potential losses and matching them to the right product and cover amount.

1.3.4 Debt management and counselling

Borrowing uses tomorrow's income today — future income must be set aside to repay it, which can stress even essential spending. The asset funded matters: an appreciating asset like property (which can also earn rent) strengthens wealth, while a depreciating asset like a car adds convenience but ongoing cost. Advisers help decide what to fund via debt, how much to borrow, how repayment is provided for, and how to avoid a debt trap.

1.3.5 Investment Planning and Asset Allocation

This is about funding the goals identified earlier. The adviser doesn't pick individual stocks or bonds — instead, it's a top-down asset allocation exercise: how much goes into which asset class to deliver the required return within the client's risk preference.

1.3.6 Tax Planning

Taxes affect how much a client can save, the return earned, and therefore the final corpus. Post-tax returns, taxability of dividends/rent/interest, and holding-period rules all matter — but tax efficiency should guide, not drive, the decision. The basis remains the client's requirements and risk appetite.

1.3.7 Retirement Planning

Many people under-plan retirement, assuming provident fund, superannuation and gratuity will be enough — often they aren't. Retirement planning leans heavily on time value of money, inflation and compounding. Pre-retirement, portfolios shift to less volatile assets; post-retirement, focus moves to whether investment and pension income covers expenses, with the corpus still monitored and rebalanced through the retirement years.

1.3.8 Estate Planning

Estate planning covers the transfer of wealth to heirs, charities or other beneficiaries — legally and tax-efficiently. Some tools, like gifts, are exercised during one's lifetime; others, like wills, take effect only after death. Advisers help clients choose the right tools and complete documentation correctly.

1.4

Concept of asset, liabilities and net worth

Income funds current expenses; the portion set aside for the future is savings. Savings are put to work in assets — broadly physical (real estate, gold — tangible, natural inflation hedges, but illiquid and lightly regulated) or financial (deposits, equities, bonds — a claim on benefits, typically standardised, liquid and easier to compare). Loans used to buy assets create a liability — a repayment obligation against future income.

1.6

Financial advisory and execution

When someone selling financial products earns commission from the manufacturer, there's a built-in conflict of interest — they may push higher-commission products over suitable ones (mis-selling). SEBI's regulatory response: separate advice (paid by the client) from distribution (paid by commission). Advisers offering advice for a fee must register with SEBI and cannot also earn from product producers.

Regulatory note

Under SEBI's amended Investment Adviser Regulations, an individual Investment Adviser must choose either advisory or distribution — not both. A non-individual adviser must segregate the two at the group level with an arm's-length structure. Individual advisers may still offer execution, but only through direct schemes/products, with no consideration received at the adviser's group or family level.
