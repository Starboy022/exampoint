Operational Aspects of Investment Management

Behind every piece of investment advice sits a machinery of operations — the paperwork, identity checks, accounts and settlement plumbing that actually get money invested and returned safely. This chapter is the adviser's operations manual: who can invest and how they are on-boarded, PAN and the KYC process (including e-KYC, IPV/VIPV, uniform KYC and the CERSAI/CKYCR registry), dematerialisation and rematerialisation of securities, the Power of Attorney, account opening for non-residents (NRE/NRO/FCNR, repatriation, PINS), ongoing folio maintenance (change of address/name/status, lien, transmission, nomination, assignment), special investor categories (minors and minor-turned-major), payment instruments (cheques, NEFT/RTGS, ASBA, third-party rules under the PMLA), the documentation for advice and the new MITC, and finally investing in mutual funds through the stock exchange platform.

17.1

Investors and the investing process

Investors in the securities markets split into two broad camps. Individual investors — resident individuals competent to contract, minors through guardians, HUFs, NRIs and Foreign Portfolio Investors — invest their own or borrowed funds, in small ticket sizes, spread out geographically. Institutional investors — companies, trusts, charitable organisations, societies, pension/insurance funds and banks — must follow a formal process: the apex body (Board or Trustees) approves decisions against an approved asset-allocation pattern, and authorised signatories execute the documentation.

Accredited Investors

SEBI's "Accredited Investor" concept covers individuals, sole proprietorships, HUFs, trusts, partnerships and companies that meet prescribed criteria on net-worth, income or assets under management. They are expected to have a greater ability and inclination for risk — so they get access to products that meet that profile.

On-boarding, terms of offer & regulatory pre-requisites

Investors must fulfil mandatory requirements before investing. Some are imposed by regulation (being KYC compliant, quoting PAN); others are product-specific and set out in the terms of offer — which can restrict who invests (e.g. NRIs cannot invest in post-office small savings), set minimum/maximum amounts, or impose age/health conditions.

Since January 1, 2016, payment for a public-issue application must be made only via the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility — an authorisation to the bank to block the money and release it only on allotment. SEBI has since added UPI with the block-funds (ASBA) facility for retail applications through intermediaries.

Mandatory investor information & the folio

An application is invalid without its mandatory fields — name, signature, address, bank account details, PAN and KYC status. For an adviser, risk profiling is an additional must-do that should never be missed. The first holder is the anchor: all payouts (dividend, interest, redemption), the tax benefit/liability and all correspondence go to the first holder, even though every joint holder's name is kept on record.

On a valid application, the provider (or its R&T agent) allots a unique folio / account number that must be quoted on every subsequent transaction. Additional investments in an existing folio can be made with a transaction slip needing only minimum details — no fresh PAN/KYC proof.

👥 Up to 3 joint holders

  • Mutual funds, shares, bonds, demat accounts
  • Post Office Savings Bank accounts
  • Most company deposits

👤 Maximum 2 holders / sole holder

  • Bank FD, Senior Citizens Savings Scheme, NSC → max 2
  • NPS account → held in the sole name of the subscriber
  • A minor's investment → minor is sole/first holder, no joint holders

17.2

PAN and the KYC process

The Permanent Account Number (PAN), issued by the Income Tax authorities, is the single identification number for capital-market transactions. Apply on Form 49A; where PAN is mandatory but unavailable, submit a declaration in Form 60/61. Section 114B of the Income Tax Rules lists transactions that mandatorily require PAN.

Not everything needs PAN, though. Try the explorer — pick a transaction to see whether PAN is mandatory or exempt. The recurring threshold is ₹50,000.

The micro-investment PAN exemption

Investments up to ₹50,000 in aggregate under all schemes of a fund house (reckoned on a rolling 12-month period or a financial year) are exempt from PAN — but only for individual investors; HUFs and non-individuals are not eligible. Such investors enclose a KYC acknowledgement quoting a 'PAN Exempt KYC' Reference Number and hold only one such number. Cash MF investments up to ₹50,000 per fund per year are also allowed for those without a PAN.

The KYC process — from the PMLA to CERSAI

KYC norms flow from the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002 — the identity of those entering financial transactions must be known and verified. KYC verifies proof of identity (a photo document: passport, driving licence, voter ID, Aadhaar) and proof of address, and also the PAN where available. For a first-time investor, In-Person Verification (IPV) is mandatory.

Technology has reshaped this. SEBI's circular of April 24, 2020 enables online KYC via eSign (under the IT Act framework), DigiLocker e-documents and electronic signature — details verified through OTP (mobile/email), UIDAI authentication (Aadhaar), the online tax database (PAN) and a penny-drop (bank account). KRA & uniform KYC
From January 1, 2012, one KYC works across MFs, DPs, brokers, PMS, VCFs. The KYC form has two parts — common identity/address, plus intermediary-specific fields. Enabled by the KYC Registration Agency (KRA).
CERSAI / CKYCR
Authorised (notification Nov 26, 2015) as the Central KYC Records Registry under PML Rules. De-duplicates records, issues a unique KYC Identifier, serves RBI, SEBI, IRDAI & PFRDA. RIs upload records for accounts opened on/after Aug 1, 2016.
eSign
Online electronic signature letting an Aadhaar holder digitally sign and forward a document. Operated under the Second Schedule of the Information Technology Act.
VIPV
Video In-Person Verification: live, clear and still, investor recognisable (face uncovered), random questions/OTP, must match the Aadhaar photo, and be tamper-proof with date/time stamping.
IPV waiver
IPV/VIPV is not required when KYC uses Aadhaar authentication/UIDAI, or when the form is submitted online with documents via DigiLocker or another online-verifiable source.
Inclusive KYC (PwD)
SEBI circular May 23, 2025 — intermediaries must offer assistive digital services so digital KYC is accessible to persons with disabilities, including the visually impaired.

17.3

Dematerialisation & rematerialisation of securities

A depository holds investors' securities in electronic form — analogous to a bank holding funds. The Depositories Act, 1996 made this possible; India has two depositories, NSDL and CDSL. All public issues above ₹10 crore must be issued only in demat form. Dematerialised securities are fungible — a share loses its distinctive certificate/folio number and is identified only by the 12-character ISIN and the number of shares held.

Dematerialisation: the investor lodges the Dematerialisation Request Form (DRF) and physical certificates with the DP → a DRN is generated → the R&T agent verifies, mutilates the certificates ("Surrendered for Dematerialisation") and the depository credits the demat account. No stamp duty is payable.

📥 Dematerialisation

  • Physical → electronic; via the DRF and DRN
  • Certificates mutilated & destroyed; entered in the Register of Destroyed Certificates
  • No stamp duty payable
  • Physical transfer of shares discontinued from 1 April 2019; DRFs stored ≥ 5 years

📤 Rematerialisation

  • Electronic → physical; via the RRF
  • R&T agent assigns a new certificate number & distinctive numbers
  • Beneficial owners added to the Register of Members; depository removed to that extent
  • R&T agent ensures revenue stamps are affixed before dispatch

17.4

Power of Attorney

An investor can empower someone they trust to transact on their behalf by executing a Power of Attorney (PoA) — commonly used by non-residents or by investors who let brokers/advisers manage their investments. A PoA has two parties: the grantor (primary investor granting the rights) and the holder / attorney (authorised to act). Both must comply with PAN and KYC norms, and the grantor can still operate the account after granting a PoA.

What a PoA holder CANNOT do

A PoA holder can do normal transactions — purchase, payment, sale, settlement, redemption — but cannot appoint or change a nominee, and cannot open or close a bank or demat account (those need the account holders' signatures). And since minors cannot enter valid contracts, there can be no PoA for a minor — the guardian plays that role instead.

Tap a PoA concept

A General PoA is broad; a Specific/Limited PoA is confined to designated acts. Execution formalities differ in India vs abroad.
General PoA
Very broad — banking, filing tax returns, buying/selling/managing property, entering contracts, settling claims. Used when the investor cannot act (travel, ill-health).
Specific / Limited PoA
Confined to a specific act or acts. Be very clear about the powers granted, since it is limited to what the document designates.
Executed in India
Typed on non-judicial stamp paper, stamped per state rules, signed by the grantor on all pages, by both parties on the last page, and notarised where required.
Executed abroad
May be on plain paper, attested by an Indian embassy official or a notary abroad, signed on all pages by the grantor, then sent to India for the holder to sign and be stamped/notarised if required.

17.5

Account opening process for non-residents

An NRI is a citizen of India (or person of Indian origin) who is not a resident of India. Residential status is decided under Section 6 of the Income Tax Act — an individual is resident if in India for 182 days or more in the year, or 60 days or more in the year and 365 days or more in the preceding 4 years (the 60-day test relaxes for citizens/PIOs visiting India). Under FEMA (Notification 13, May 3, 2000), an NRI is a person resident outside India who is a citizen of India.

The key operational issue is repatriation — can the proceeds go back abroad? It depends on the source of funds, tracked through the bank account. Try the explorer:

Funds remitted from abroad into an NRE account (foreign-currency source) can be freely taken back out without limit.
Why rupee-sourced proceeds are non-repatriable

Since the rupee is not fully convertible on the capital account, RBI norms don't permit rupee investments to be freely converted and taken out. So funds from an NRE/FCNR account (or a draft on a foreign bank with FIRC) are freely repatriable; funds from an NRO account are repatriable only up to USD 1 million per financial year. From January 2016, a FATCA/CRS self-declaration is mandatory for all Indian and NRI investors.

Tap an NRI account / scheme

NRIs need distinct accounts for repatriable vs non-repatriable money, and a PINS account for trading in listed shares.
NRE account
Non-Resident External — foreign-currency source; proceeds freely repatriable.
NRO account
Non-Resident Ordinary — rupee income source; proceeds repatriable only up to USD 1 million / financial year.
FCNR account
Foreign Currency Non-Resident — holds foreign currency; source proceeds freely repatriable.
RFC account
Resident Foreign Currency — for a returning NRI to transfer NRE/FCNR(B) balances and keep receiving foreign currency.
PINS account
RBI's Portfolio Investment Scheme — mandatory for NRIs/PIOs trading listed shares on an exchange. One NRE(PIS) + one NRO(PIS) only; separate even if an NRE account exists.

NRI demat & trading accounts. No RBI permission is needed to open an NRI demat account, but separate accounts are needed for repatriable and non-repatriable securities; short selling and currency/commodity trading are not allowed. Under PINS, an individual NRI cannot buy more than 5% of a company's paid-up capital, within an overall 10% for all NRIs (raisable to 24% by a General Body resolution).

17.6

Folio maintenance, lien & transmission

Accounts need upkeep — changes of address, name, status, bank mandate — plus marking a lien, transmission on death, and nomination. For capital-market records the golden rule is that a change of address/name/status must be carried out in the KYC records at the KRA (via the KYC details change form); merely informing the AMC, RTA, DP, broker or PMS is not sufficient. The KRA then propagates the change to every entity the investor deals with.

Marking a lien

Investors can pledge securities as collateral; the lender creates a lien. The investor cannot redeem or transfer securities under lien until the lien holder authorises unmarking in writing — but dividends/benefits still go to the investor unless specifically barred. "Invoking" the lien means the lien holder sells/redeems the pledged securities to recover dues; there is no transfer of securities to the lien holder.

Transmission — who inherits?

Transmission passes investments to those entitled on the death of the holder. Who receives them depends on how the account was held and whether there was a nomination. Work the decision tree:

Simplified transmission thresholds

A death certificate is required in every transmission. Below a threshold, heavy documents (succession certificate, probate) may be waived — a death certificate, indemnity bond and NOC being adequate. The simplified threshold is ₹5 lakh for physical securities and ₹15 lakh for dematerialised securities. On the death of an HUF Karta, the investment passes to the new Karta against an indemnity bond signed by all remaining co-parceners.

Nomination & assignment

Investors subscribing to MF units on/after October 1, 2022 must nominate or opt out via a signed declaration; nomination is mandatory for singly-held MF/demat folios and can be made only by individuals (a PoA holder cannot nominate). Per revisions effective 1 March 2025, an investor can nominate up to 10 persons. Assignment transfers rights to another — for insurance, an assignment to a lender is absolute (unchangeable), while one in case of early death can be conditional.

17.7

Change in status of special investor categories

Minors, NRIs and investors acting through a constituted attorney need extra documentation because of differences in taxation, mode of operation, or restrictions.

Minors & the minor-turned-major event

A minor (under 18) invests through a guardian — the minor is sole/first holder with no joint holders; the guardian completes KYC as if investing themselves and provides the guardian's PAN. Guardianship can change on death (notarised death certificate) or mutual consent (consent/NOC or court order under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 / Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956).

The day a minor turns major

On the date of majority, the folio is frozen for the guardian's operation and all standing instructions (SIP, STP, SWP) are suspended until the minor-turned-major documents are received. The minor must complete fresh KYC; a new demat account is opened (a minor's demat is single-name only) and securities transferred; and the PAN is resubmitted for a new card with the same number but the new adult signature.

🏠 NRI → Resident

  • Can no longer operate NRO/NRE/FCNR(B); open a Resident Rupee Account
  • Optionally open an RFC account to hold transferred foreign-currency balances
  • New Resident demat & trading accounts; balances transferred, NRI accounts closed
  • File KYC change form with the KRA

✈️ Resident → NRI

  • Can no longer operate the resident savings account; open NRE/NRO
  • FDs converted to NRO FDs; new NRI demat & trading accounts (non-repatriable)
  • Can keep securities bought as a resident, on a non-repatriable basis
  • Once flagged NRI, TDS applies on gains at redemption

Adding/deleting a name. A bank account allows name addition/deletion with documents and KYC. A mutual fund usually does not allow adding a name (it amounts to a transfer of units) — the solution is to open a new folio with the desired holding and move the units after closing the existing one.

17.8

Payment instruments

All capital-market payments must be routed through the first holder's bank account. Accepted modes include local & at-par cheques, demand drafts, post-dated cheques (for SIPs only), electronic modes (NACH/ACH, RTGS, NEFT), standing instructions, limited cash, and ASBA. Outstation cheques and post-dated cheques (except for SIPs) are not permitted.

Tap a payment instrument

Watch the thresholds: RTGS is for ₹2 lakh and above; NEFT has no minimum; IFSC is 11 characters, MICR is 9 digits.
ASBA
Blocks money in the bank account for a primary issue; debited only on allotment. Advantages: money keeps earning interest, and no refunds needed.
RTGS
For transactions of ₹2 lakh and above; settled continuously in real time. Needs the beneficiary's IFSC (11 characters).
NEFT
No minimum amount; settled in timed batches. Also needs the IFSC. Charges range ₹5–₹25 per transaction.
Cheques
Local & at-par cheques accepted; outstation and post-dated cheques not permitted (post-dated allowed only for SIPs, matching the SIP date).
Cash (MF)
Since Sept 2012, cash MF investment up to ₹50,000 per investor per MF per financial year — but redemptions/dividends are paid only through banking channels.
Third-party payments & fraud prevention

Payment must come from the investor's own (first holder's) account — third-party payments are generally not accepted, to prevent fraud. Exceptions (with a third-party declaration and PAN/KYC compliance): grandparents/parents up to ₹50,000 per transaction for a minor, an employer paying for an employee via payroll, and a custodian paying for FPIs. Under the PMLA, cash MF subscriptions are capped at ₹50,000/investor/MF/year, but redemptions and dividends flow only through banking channels.

17.9–17.10

Documentation for advice, the MITC & the exchange platform

The SEBI (Investment Adviser) Regulations, 2013 prescribe the documentation that makes advice complete: a signed client agreement (roles, scope, remuneration, good faith), collected client information, risk profiling and suitability, a documented product short-listing process, and records maintained for five years (if electronic, digitally signed). SEBI now mandates including the Most Important Terms and Conditions (MITC) in the advisory agreement.

MITC — the headline protections

The IA only accepts fees (never funds/securities on the client's behalf); does not guarantee returns; and cannot execute any trade without the client's specific positive consent on every trade. Assured/guaranteed-return schemes are prohibited. Fees are capped for individual/HUF clients; advance fees ≤ one year; cash is not allowed (cheque/bank transfer/UPI, or CeFCoM run by BSE); on premature termination the client gets a proportionate refund (IA retains at most a one-quarter breakage fee). The IA advises direct (non-commission) plans, avoids distribution to the client/family, and shall never ask for the client's login credentials or OTPs. Grievances escalate: IA → SEBI SCORES → Smart ODR.

Investing in mutual funds through the stock exchange platform

Recognised exchanges let investors buy and sell MF units through their platforms — BSE StAR MF and NSE NMF II. A distributor must be registered with AMFI to use the infrastructure; units are purchased from and redeemed with the AMCs directly. The distributor only handles the transaction — the pay-in/pay-out of money and units flows through the exchange infrastructure and the clearing corporation, eliminating counterparty risk. SIP, STP, SWP and switch transactions are all supported, in demat or physical form.

For the adviser, the transaction feed shows the client's entire MF portfolio and transaction status in one place, allows family members to be clubbed, generates holding statements on demand, and needs almost no paperwork — improving transparency and service.
