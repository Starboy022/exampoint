Key Regulations

An investment adviser operates inside a dense lattice of law. At the base sit two foundational statutes — the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 and the SEBI Act, 1992 — on which SEBI has built the conduct code that governs the market: the PFUTP, Intermediaries and Insider Trading Regulations. Above all of these, for the adviser, stands the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013 — the profession's rulebook, from who may register through fiduciary duty, risk profiling, suitability, record-keeping and the code of conduct. Wrapping around it are the PMLA, 2002 anti-money-laundering regime and a shelf of other acts — FEMA, the Indian Contract Act, the Guardian and Wards Act, the Negotiable Instruments Act, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, and FATCA/CRS. This chapter maps the whole lattice and shows, through SEBI's own case studies, what happens when an adviser steps outside it.

18.1–18.2

The two foundations — SCRA 1956 & the SEBI Act 1992

Two statutes hold up the entire edifice. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA) exists "to prevent undesirable transactions in securities by regulating the business of dealing therein." A securities transaction between two persons is, at bottom, a contract — and SCRA is the law that specifically governs that contract, giving the Central Government jurisdiction over three things: stock exchanges (through recognition and continued supervision), contracts in securities, and the listing of securities.

The SEBI Act, 1992 then established the Board "to protect the interests of investors in securities and to promote the development of, and to regulate, the securities market." Watch the architecture assemble:

The two enabling statutes empower SEBI, which in turn registers and regulates intermediaries and lays down the market-conduct regulations — with the Investment Advisers Regulations, 2013 sitting on top for advisers specifically.

Without prejudice to that broad mandate, the SEBI Act specifically provides for measures including registering and regulating intermediaries (brokers, merchant bankers, portfolio managers, investment advisers and others), depositories, FPIs and credit-rating agencies; regulating mutual funds and collective investment schemes; prohibiting fraudulent and unfair trade practices; prohibiting insider trading; regulating substantial acquisition and takeovers; promoting investor education; and calling for information, inspections and audits. It also empowers SEBI to impose penalties and start adjudication against defaulting intermediaries.

18.3–18.5

Market-conduct rules — fraud, the code of conduct & insider trading

Three regulations set the boundaries of fair play.

PFUTP Regulations, 2003 — the definition of fraud

The SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices) Regulations, 2003 prohibit fraudulent, unfair and manipulative trade practices. Fraud is defined inclusively — any act, expression, omission or concealment committed to induce another to deal in securities. Crucially, whether there is any wrongful gain or avoidance of loss is inconsequential to determining that fraud occurred.

The five instances of fraud (PFUTP)

(a) A wilful misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact so another acts to his detriment; (b) a suggestion of a fact which is untrue, by one who does not believe it true; (c) active concealment of a fact by a person with knowledge/belief of it; (d) a promise made without any intention of performing it; (e) a representation, whether true or false, made in a reckless and careless manner.

Intermediaries Regulations, 2008 — the code of conduct

The SEBI (Intermediaries) Regulations, 2008 lay down a code of conduct: protect investor interests and render best possible advice; observe high standards of integrity and professionalism; exercise due diligence and not collude with other intermediaries against investors; not increase fees without advance notice; be prompt in disbursing income; make adequate, non-misleading disclosures; make no exaggerated claims; and not divulge confidential client information without prior permission (except where the law requires it).

Prohibition of Insider Trading Regulations, 2015

An "insider" is any person who is either a connected person or is in possession of / has access to unpublished price sensitive information (UPSI). Trading by an insider on information not in the public domain gives an undue advantage and affects market integrity — the Regulations define who counts as an insider, what is prohibited, and the fair-conduct policies that listed companies and intermediaries must adopt.

18.6

The IA Regulations, 2013 — who is an "investment adviser"?

SEBI notified the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013 to regulate the activity of providing investment advisory services by independent advisors, distributors, banks and others. Everything starts with the definitions.

"Investment advice" means advice relating to investing in, purchasing, selling or otherwise dealing in securities, and advice on an investment portfolio containing securities — written, oral or otherwise — and includes financial planning. But two things are carved out:

✅ Inside "investment advice"

  • Personalised advice to buy/sell/hold securities
  • Advice on a securities portfolio
  • Financial planning — analysing the client's situation, identifying goals, recommending strategies
  • Written, oral, or any other means of communication

❌ Expressly excluded

  • Advice via newspaper/magazine/electronic media widely available to the public
  • Trading calls — intraday, ultra-short-duration, non-delivery-based, non-personalised recommendations

An "investment adviser" is any person who, for consideration, is engaged in the business of providing investment advice — including a part-time IA or anyone who holds himself out as an IA. A part-time IA also runs another business/employment, which must be unrelated to securities and must neither handle client money nor advise on products for investment.

18.6.2–18.6.4

Registration, exemptions & qualifications

No person shall act as, or hold himself out as, an investment adviser without a certificate of registration from SEBI — and that certificate is valid until suspended or cancelled (no annual renewal). SEBI weighs whether the applicant and its associated persons are appropriately qualified and certified, are "fit and proper," have the necessary infrastructure, and — for a bank/NBFC — have RBI permission and apply through a subsidiary or separately identifiable department/division.

Qualification & certification (Regulation 7)

An individual IA / principal officer must hold, at all times, a professional qualification or a graduate/post-graduate degree or PG diploma (min two years) in a relevant field — or a PG Program in the Securities Market (Investment Advisory) from NISM (≥1 year) or a CFA Charter. Persons associated with advice need at least a graduate degree in any discipline. Everyone must hold the relevant NISM certification at all times, renewing it before the existing one expires.

18.6.7–18.6.16

The advisory process — obligations that make advice lawful

At the heart of the IA Regulations is a chain of duties. The adviser acts in a fiduciary capacity, must disclose conflicts as they arise, and cannot take any consideration from anyone other than the client for the products advised on. Tap each obligation:

Tap an adviser obligation

These run in sequence: know the client → assess risk → check suitability → disclose → keep records → segregate → implement only via direct plans.
Fiduciary & 15-day rule
Act in the client's best interest; disclose conflicts as they arise. Do not trade on own account contrary to advice for 15 days (unless a revised assessment is given ≥24 hours in advance).
Risk profiling
Obtain age, objectives, horizon, income, assets/liabilities, risk appetite. Tools must be fit for purpose; questions fair, clear, not misleading — no double negatives, no leading questions. Communicate & update periodically.
Suitability
Advice appropriate to the risk profile; documented selection process; a reasonable basis that it meets objectives and the client can bear the risk. Complex products need extra assessment.
Disclosures
Material information about itself, disciplinary history, holdings in advised products, conflicts, track record, warnings/disclaimers, out-of-SEBI-purview products, and extent of AI use.
Records (5 years)
KYC, risk profiling, suitability, agreements, advice, signed rationale, client register (with PAN), communications — kept ≥5 years (digitally signed if electronic). Yearly audit by a CA/CS/CMA; functional website.
Segregation
An individual IA cannot provide distribution. A non-individual IA keeps client-level segregation at group level: a client is either advisory or distribution, never both within the group.
Implementation
May implement advice, but only through direct schemes/products, with no commission/referral fee at group/family level and no implementation fee. The client is under no obligation to use it.
Fees
Advance fees, if agreed, cannot exceed one year. Fee limit/mode/refund/advance/breakage rules apply only to individual & HUF clients that are not accredited investors.
The 15-day own-account rule in action

An IA advises a client to buy Stock X. For 15 days the IA must not sell Stock X from its own account (i.e. act contrary to its advice). If within those 15 days the IA genuinely believes the situation has changed, it may transact — but only after giving the client a revised assessment at least 24 hours in advance.

18.6.17–18.6.22

Code of conduct, Investor Charter, administration & IFSC

The Third Schedule of the IA Regulations sets a code of conduct binding on all IAs at all times: honesty and fairness, diligence, capabilities, seeking and protecting information about clients, adequate information to clients, fair and reasonable charges, avoiding/disclosing conflicts of interest, compliance with all regulatory requirements, and senior-management responsibility. SEBI further requires restriction of free trials, proper risk profiling with client consent, receiving fees only through banking channels, and displaying complaint status on the website.

Investor Charter (SEBI circular, June 2, 2025)

IAs must display the charter on their website and in offices, provide it at onboarding, and publicly disclose monthly complaint data by the 7th of the succeeding month in the SEBI-specified format. The charter's Do's: deal only with SEBI-registered IAs, verify registration, pay fees only via banking channels (or the Centralised Fee Collection Mechanism), insist on risk profiling. Its Don'ts: don't fall for assured/exorbitant returns, don't give funds to the IA, and never share login credentials for trading, demat or bank accounts.

Administration. SEBI recognised a wholly-owned stock-exchange subsidiary to administer and supervise IAs. The parent exchange must have ≥15 years' existence, ≥₹200 crore net-worth, nationwide terminals, an arbitration mechanism, and ISCs in ≥20 cities. BSE Administration & Supervision Limited was granted this recognition — it supervises IAs (on-site/off-site), redresses grievances, takes administrative action (warnings, referral to SEBI), monitors via periodic reports and maintains the IA database. Separately, the SEBI (IFSC) Guidelines, 2015 (issued March 27, 2015) extend all IA Regulation provisions to advisers operating in an IFSC.

18.7

The Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002

Money laundering means disguising financial assets so they can be used without detecting the illegal activity that produced them — transforming criminal proceeds into funds with an apparently legal source. The PMLA, 2002 prevents this and provides for confiscation of property involved.

Section 3 — the offence. Whoever directly/indirectly attempts, assists, is a party to, or is involved in any process connected with proceeds of crime — including its concealment, possession, acquisition, use, or projecting/claiming it as untainted property — is guilty of money-laundering.
Section 12 — record-keeping. Every reporting entity (banking company, financial institution, intermediary, or designated business/profession) must maintain records of all transactions for 5 years from the date of transaction, furnish prescribed information to the Director, and keep identity/beneficial-owner documents for 5 years after the relationship ends / account is closed, whichever is later. All such information is kept confidential.
Section 12AA — enhanced due diligence. Before each specified transaction, verify identity (via Aadhaar authentication), examine ownership/financial position and source of funds, and record the purpose. If the client fails these, the transaction shall not be carried out. Suspicious transactions trigger increased monitoring.
SEBI AML/CFT guidelines & the KRA

SEBI's Anti-Money Laundering (AML) & Combating the Financing of Terrorism (CFT) guidelines under the PMLA apply to all SEBI-registered intermediaries (and their overseas branches/subsidiaries), aligned with the FATF recommendations. SEBI also issued the KYC Registration Agency (KRA) Regulations, 2011 — a KRA is a company that obtains and holds the client's KYC documents from the intermediary. Every individual investing in securities markets must be KYC compliant.

Other acts & SEBI's case studies

A handful of other statutes touch the advisory profession. Tap each to see what it governs:

Tap an act

Know the one-line purpose of each — the exam tests which act does what.
FEMA 1999
Consolidates the law on foreign exchange, external trade & payments. FPI = investment by a person resident outside India that is <10% of a listed company's post-issue paid-up equity.
Indian Contract Act 1872
Foundation for all trade contracts. A contract [Sec 2(h)] is "an agreement enforceable by law". Needs offer/acceptance, free consent, lawful object, capacity, intent.
Guardian & Wards Act 1890
Protects minors & secures their property. A minor is under 18 (Indian Majority Act 1875); a guardian cares for the minor's person/property; a ward is that minor.
Negotiable Instruments Act 1881
Defines promissory note (unconditional undertaking), bill of exchange (unconditional order) & cheque (a bill drawn on a banker, payable on demand).
Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code 2016
A single, time-bound law for insolvency resolution of corporates, firms & individuals. Organised into five parts.
FATCA / CRS
FATCA = US law to catch tax evasion by US persons. All Indian/NRI investors file a self-declaration; RFIs upload self-certifications to the KRA from 1 July 2024; reportable accounts reported in Form 61B.
18.9 Case studies — what SEBI's orders teach

Case 1 (Registration): an entity provided trading tips and advice for fees without any SEBI certificate. Takeaway — anyone doing advisory work must register; an unregistered IA endangers investors and denies them grievance/arbitration remedies. Case 2 (False credentials): a proprietor obtained registration with a forged experience certificate and promised a lucrative return in 4 months. SEBI restrained him from the securities market and cancelled his registration — false documents breach the conditions of certificate, and promising exorbitant returns violates the duty to protect investors.
