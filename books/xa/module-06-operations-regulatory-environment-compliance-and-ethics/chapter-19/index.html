Ethical Issues

Law tells an adviser what they may do; ethics tells them what they should. In money matters — where a client's goals and life are at stake — conduct of the highest ethical standard is what builds the trust the entire advisory industry runs on, and trust takes years to build and moments to lose. This chapter works from first principles: what ethics is, why the Client First principle sits at the centre of the business, the recurring grievance areas that betray it, how to resolve genuine ethical dilemmas, and the fiduciary duty of utmost good faith that binds every adviser. It closes with SEBI's Do's and Don'ts for investors, the yearly compliance audit, and how the world's regulators — the US SEC and Australia's Section 961B — codify the same idea: put the client first.

19.1–19.2

Ethics & why ethical conduct matters

Ethics is a set of moral principles or rules of conduct that guide our behaviour when it affects others — an area dealing with what is good for society and for individuals. The word comes from the Greek ethos, meaning custom, habit or character. Widely acknowledged fundamental ethical principles include honesty, fairness, diligence, care and respect for others.

Acting within the law only ensures nothing illegal is done. Ethics goes one step beyond — it brings into play what is right or wrong, arising from a person's basic values and character built over a lifetime. So an adviser can stay perfectly legal and still act unethically: for instance, collecting a fee and then avoiding the aftersales service a client's situation clearly needs.

The law is only the floor. Ethics rises above it to what is right — and consistent ethical conduct is what earns trust, the foundation of every advisory relationship.

This is why ethical conduct is central to the business. An adviser who puts the client's interest above everything else — the Client First principle — is on the correct ethical path and, crucially, has no conflict of interest. If clients sense the adviser is looking out only for themselves, confidence collapses. Trust sits at the centre of everything an adviser does: it can take years to build but moments to lose. Being proactive — suggesting what a client needs before they have to ask — is a big long-term positive, and living up to the role of a trustworthy custodian of someone's money is what makes an adviser a genuine role model.

19.3

Ethical issues in providing financial advice

A financial intermediary channelizes domestic savings into investment vehicles — helping issuers mobilise resources while helping investors earn a reasonable return, by taking the most suitable product to the retail investor. As Zig Ziglar put it: "You can have everything in life you want, if you will just help other people get what they want." Yet advisers working to targets sometimes lose sight of suitability. No relative valuation across the product universe — e.g. selling Small Cap Funds while the segment is rallying, which has to be seen in perspective.
Churning
Frequent switching between products to earn more commission. A reason SEBI mandated advisers can't distribute — though it can persist in other channels.
Not disclosing risks
Highlighting only good features (misrepresentation) — e.g. a Distress Debt Fund (AIF) carries higher default risk that must be communicated.
Poor aftersales service
Poor service, or deviating from what was promised earlier — a clear ethical failure once the fee is collected.
The remedy is nearly always the same

Explain the product's features and make sure the client has understood them before investing; conduct risk profiling; educate the client or point them to relevant sources; and communicate risks and uncertainties, not just the good features. Keep the investor at the centre, with their interest uppermost.

19.4

The ethical dilemma

An ethical dilemma is a situation where a person must decide between two alternatives, neither of which is superior. The option chosen sends a signal — and a series of such signals reinforces the client's perception of the adviser's ethical standards. A pure ethical dilemma is one where both alternatives are against some ethical principle, so any choice involves some violation.

Four ways to tackle a dilemma

1. Deeper analysis — often the "dilemma" turns out to be a clear case with one correct course. 2. Guiding principles — take the step that does the highest amount of good for all involved, or the one that does the least amount of damage. 3. Reframe — reconsider the whole problem differently to surface new solutions. And remember: in a world of social media, clients bring many ethical and unethical views to the adviser for clarity — justification is part of the job, so ethical behaviour is an imperative.

19.5

Fiduciary responsibility of investment advisers

Investment Advisers are fiduciaries — they must act in the best interest of their clients, rising above every conflict of interest to keep the client at the forefront of each step. Because an adviser acts on behalf of another person to manage their financial assets, the relationship demands the principle of utmost good faith — uberrimae fidei in Latin. There can be no profit made at the cost of the client, and the adviser must never take unfair advantage of the client's trust.

Disclose even the perception of conflict

Full disclosure is essential whenever there is a conflict of interest — and disclosure should also be made when there can merely be a perception of conflict, so that trust and faith are maintained at all times. If Product A pays the adviser a bigger commission but Product B suits the client better, the fiduciary duty is to recommend B, disclose the conflict, and not let the commission drive the advice.

✅ SEBI's Do's

  • Deal only with SEBI-registered Investment Advisers; check the registration number & a valid certificate
  • Pay only advisory fees, through banking channels, and keep duly signed receipts
  • Always ask for risk profiling; insist advice is based on it and on available alternatives

⛔ SEBI's Don'ts

  • Don't deal with unregistered entities; don't go by the number of social-media followers
  • Don't fall for stock tips offered under the pretext of advice
  • Don't give your money to the Investment Adviser for investment
  • Don't fall for indicative / exorbitant / assured returns; don't let greed override reason
  • Don't get carried away by luring advertisements or rumours; don't transact only on calls/messages
  • Don't fall prey to limited-period discounts, incentives or gifts; don't rush into unsuitable investments

19.7

Addressing annual audit observations

A compliance audit must be conducted by investment advisers on a yearly basis, carried out by a Practising Chartered Accountant. Where the audit throws up any material observations, the adviser should disclose them to clients — an extension of the fiduciary duty of full disclosure and transparency.

Frequency & auditor. The compliance audit is conducted yearly by a Practising Chartered Accountant — not by the adviser and not only on complaint.
Disclose material observations. If there are any material audit observations, the adviser should disclose them to clients rather than suppress them.

19.8

Global best practices

Regulators worldwide put a premium on ethical behaviour. Despite different guidelines, the common threads are disclosure of conflicts and disciplinary matters, a written code of ethics, and acting in the client's best interest.

🇺🇸 US — SEC

  • Disclose a financial condition that would impair the adviser's ability to meet contractual commitments
  • Disclose certain disciplinary events within the past 10 years that are materially important
  • Adopt and implement written policies identifying conflicts and compliance risks
  • Adopt and enforce a written code of ethics reflecting fiduciary duties

🇦🇺 Australia

  • Act with competence, honesty, integrity and fairness
  • Strict penalties — including bans and disqualification — for violations
  • Must satisfy the 'best interests' standard of Section 961B, Corporations Act 2001
  • Identify the client's situation/objectives/needs, ensure info is complete, and only recommend a product after thoroughly investigating the most appropriate options

The SEC Code of Ethics — tap an element

At a minimum, an SEC-registered adviser's written code of ethics must contain each of these.

Set a minimum standard of conduct for all supervised persons.
b · Comply with Federal Securities Laws
Require supervised persons to comply with federal securities laws.
c · Personal Securities Transactions
Access persons report holdings on becoming one & at least annually, and report all personal trades at least quarterly to the CCO.
d · Pre-approval
The CCO (or designee) must pre-approve access persons' investments in IPOs or limited offerings.
e · Reporting Violations
All supervised persons must promptly report any violations of the code to the CCO or designee.
f · Distribution & Acknowledgment
Give each supervised person a copy of the code (and amendments) and obtain a written acknowledgment of receipt.
g · Recordkeeping
Keep copies of the code, records of violations & actions taken, and each person's acknowledgment of receipt.
