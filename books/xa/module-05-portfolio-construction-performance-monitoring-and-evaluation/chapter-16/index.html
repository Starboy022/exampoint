Portfolio Performance Measurement & Evaluation

The natural human tendency is to judge an investment purely by the return it earned — with little regard for the risk taken to get there. Proper performance measurement recognises both. This chapter is the adviser's measurement toolkit: the many ways to compute return (holding-period return, TWRR vs MWRR, geometric vs arithmetic mean, gross vs net, pre- vs post-tax, CAGR, cash-drag adjustment, and alpha/beta decomposition), the ways to compute risk (total & downside risk, tracking error, systematic vs unsystematic risk, beta, liquidity and credit risk), the risk-adjusted measures that combine the two (Sharpe, Treynor, Sortino, Information Ratio and M²), and finally how to evaluate performance through benchmarking, peer-group analysis and attribution.

16.1–16.2.2

Return & risk, holding-period return, TWRR vs MWRR

The central issue in performance measurement is a human bias: we fixate on the return earned and pay too little attention to the risk taken to earn it. Proper performance measurement recognises both return and risk.

The most vital statistic is the rate of return, which has many definitions — one for each purpose. Always clarify the purpose first, then pick the appropriate return measure.

Holding-Period Return (HPR)

The most straightforward measure — also called total return or point-to-point return. It is the income generated plus the change in value, over the beginning value:

HPR = ( I + (E − B) ) ⁄ B  ·  I = income, E = ending value, B = beginning value

Workbook example: a portfolio worth ₹1,00,000 grows to ₹1,20,000 → HPR 20%. Add ₹5,000 of dividend/interest income → HPR 25%. It assumes income is distributed at the year end; despite this, HPR is the widely accepted starting point of performance measurement.

TWRR vs MWRR over multiple periods

Over several periods with cash flows in and out, two returns diverge:

💰 Money-Weighted Rate of Return (MWRR)

  • Same as the IRR — the rate at which cumulative contributions grow over the period
  • Depends on the timing of the cash flows
  • Workbook 5-year example → MWRR (IRR) ≈ 15.15%
  • Adviser/wealth-manager client reporting is on an MWRR basis — "what the client takes home"

⏱️ Time-Weighted Rate of Return (TWRR)

  • Compound rate of growth, unaffected by the timing of external cash flows
  • Portfolio is valued at every external cash flow; sub-period returns are chain-linked
  • Same 5-year example → TWRR ≈ 6.02% (equals the geometric return)
  • SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulation 2020 mandates TWRR for discretionary PMS disclosure (preceding 3 years)
Why TWRR for PMS but MWRR for the client?

Mutual funds have a daily NAV, so returns can be computed any way. In PMS, portfolios are customised with no NAV, so SEBI mandates TWRR to give fund-manager reporting uniformity and comparability. But money flows in and out many times over a long holding period, so adviser/client reporting uses MWRR — because ultimately the client cares about how much they actually take home.

16.2.3–16.2.5

Geometric vs arithmetic mean, gross vs net, pre- vs post-tax

Two ways to average a series of returns tell very different stories:

➕ Arithmetic Mean Return (AMR)

  • Simple average — sum the returns, divide by count
  • The best estimate of a future single year's return from a random distribution of past years
  • Choice 1 (−50%, +100%): AMR = 25%

✖️ Geometric Mean Return (GMR)

  • Compound return — chain-link wealth relatives, then take the n-th root
  • Depends only on initial and final values, not the path; the only honest way to compare long-run accumulations
  • Choice 1 (−50%, +100%): GMR = 0% — you end where you started
Same average, very different wealth

₹1,00,000 in a portfolio that goes −50% then +100% ends at exactly ₹1,00,000 (GMR 0%) — yet its AMR is 25%. A steadier +10%, +10% portfolio ends at ₹1,21,000 (GMR 10%) with a lower AMR of 10%. Over a one-year horizon AMR and GMR are identical; over longer horizons GMR ≤ AMR, with equality only when every yearly return is the same. A manager can always inflate the arithmetic average by taking more risk — so use GMR to compare long-term accumulations.

Gross vs net return. Gross return is the total return before any fees, expenses or commissions. Net return adjusts for them — it is what the investor actually makes. In the workbook PMS example a 20% gross return, after other expenses, fixed management fee, performance fee (over a 10% hurdle) and a 2% exit load, becomes a 13.88% net return (total charges ₹6,12,400 on a ₹1 crore portfolio).

Pre- vs post-tax return. Since investors sit in different tax brackets, performance is communicated pre-tax for comparability; each investor then adjusts to their own rate. What ultimately matters to the investor is the post-tax return.

Post-tax return = Pre-tax return × (1 − tax rate)  ·  Pre-tax = Post-tax ⁄ (1 − tax rate)

Example: a 5% pre-tax return with a 15% capital-gains tax → 5% × (1 − 0.15) = 4.25% post-tax.

CAGR, annualizing, cash drag & alpha/beta decomposition

CAGR (Compounded Annual Growth Rate) measures an investment's annual growth over time with compounding, assuming income is reinvested. Annualizing a multi-year return uses the same chain-link-then-take-the-root method — it is the CAGR / geometric mean:

CAGR = ( A ⁄ P )(1 ⁄ t) − 1  ·  A = closing wealth, P = opening wealth, t = years

Cash-drag adjusted return. Managers may not invest the entire contribution. Ignoring the idle cash overstates the return. Workbook example: ₹100L contributed, ₹75L in equity (earned ₹7.5L → 10% on equity), ₹25L in liquid funds at 4%. On the equity alone it looks like 10%, but on the whole ₹100L the true return is (10%×75L + 4%×25L) = ₹8.5L, i.e. 8.5% — and the manager must report on the entire portfolio including the cash drag.

Alpha & beta decomposition (via CAPM)

Portfolio returns can be split into beta return (reward for bearing market/systematic risk) and alpha return (reward for bearing non-market/unsystematic risk — a mark of skill). CAPM gives the required return; anything above it is Jensen's alpha:

Required return = Rf + β·(Rm − Rf)  ·  Alpha = Portfolio return − [ Rf + β·(Rm − Rf) ]

16.3

Risk measures

Risk is the decisive factor in selecting a portfolio manager. Risk can mean the full variability of returns (total risk) or be limited to losses / worse-than-expected outcomes (downside risk).

📊 Total risk

  • Captured by variance and standard deviation of returns
  • SD works across any portfolio type and lets similar portfolios be compared over a time frame
  • Portfolio SD is not the weighted average of individual SDs (except at perfect +1 correlation) — it depends on weights, individual SDs and, crucially, correlation

📉 Downside risk

  • Captured by semi-variance / semi-standard deviation (dispersion below the mean)
  • Target semi-variance measures dispersion below a target return
  • For symmetric returns it just tracks variance; forecasting asymmetric returns is hard, so it is not widely used in practice

Beyond volatility, the chapter names several specific risks. Tap each:

Tap a risk type

Systematic risk is measured by Beta and cannot be diversified away (only hedged); unsystematic risk can be diversified away.
Systematic / market risk
From common factors (interest rates, exchange rates, commodity prices) affecting all investments. Cannot be diversified away (only hedged). Measured by Beta. Beta return rewards bearing it.
Unsystematic risk
Sector-/company-specific risk. Can be diversified away. Alpha return is the reward for bearing it.
Tracking error
The standard deviation of the difference between portfolio and benchmark total return. Lower = closer to the index. Always computed vs the Total Returns Index. (Tracking difference is the simple point-to-point gap.)
Liquidity risk
Uncertainty from the secondary market — the difficulty/price impact of converting an asset to cash near its economic worth. T-bills ≈ none; a piece of art = high.
Credit risk
Risk that a borrower cannot repay on time. Arises in debt instruments; a deterioration in the borrower's financial condition can prevent repayment.

Beta — measuring systematic risk

Beta relates a stock/portfolio's return to the market index, reflecting sensitivity to market moves. Portfolio beta is the weighted average of the constituents' betas. β > 1 = more volatile than the index; β < 1 = less volatile.

βportfolio = Σ ( weighti × βi )  ·  e.g. 0.60×1.2 + 0.40×1.1 = 1.16

16.4

Risk-adjusted return measures

Differential returns may simply reflect more risk taken, so we must adjust return per unit of risk. The three excess-return ratios share the same numerator (Rp − Rf) but differ in the risk in the denominator:

Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) ⁄ σp  ·  Treynor = (Rp − Rf) ⁄ βp  ·  Sortino = (Rp − Rf) ⁄ semi-SD

Sharpe vs Treynor — the diversification tell

Sharpe uses total risk (SD); Treynor uses systematic risk (β). For a fully well-diversified portfolio the two give identical rankings (total risk ≈ systematic risk). For a poorly diversified portfolio, Treynor can rank it higher than Sharpe because Treynor ignores unsystematic risk. So use Sharpe for an investor who is not well-diversified overall, and Treynor for one whose wealth is already well-diversified. Any ranking difference between them is purely a diversification effect.

Information Ratio (Appraisal ratio) tells whether an observed alpha is skill or chance: IR = (Rp − Rb) ⁄ SD(Rp − Rb). The numerator is the active return; the denominator is the active risk — which is nothing but the portfolio's tracking error.

M² (Modigliani–Modigliani) levers/de-levers the portfolio (mixing with T-bills) until its volatility equals the market's, then compares the adjusted return directly with the market. Workbook: portfolio 35% (SD 42%), market 28% (SD 30%), T-bill 6% → adjusted return (30/42=0.714 in P, 0.286 in T-bills) = 0.714×35% + 0.286×6% = 26.7%, which is 1.3% below the market → the portfolio underperformed.

16.5

Performance evaluation: benchmarking & peer-group analysis

Measurement is only half the job; evaluation is relative — comparing performance to a suitable benchmark or similar portfolios to judge whether the manager added value beyond a passive index and whether the fees are justified. GIPS defines a benchmark as an independent rate of return forming an objective test of an investment strategy's implementation. A good benchmark satisfies:

Constituents & weights clearly defined Investable (passively replicable) Consistent with the portfolio's approach/style Same risk-return profile as the portfolio Performance is measurable

🛠️ Customized benchmark

  • When market indices don't fit the manager's strategy/style, build a custom investment-universe benchmark
  • Advantage: meets all the valid-benchmark criteria
  • Disadvantage: higher construction/maintenance cost than using a market index

⚠️ Benchmarking errors

  • Choosing a benchmark not representative of the portfolio (the most common error)
  • Not updating the benchmark when the investment style/exposure changes radically
  • Benchmark not investible / replicable, or its components change too much
  • Benchmark data (esp. Total Return Index) not available
Managers' universe (peer-group) analysis

A very popular evaluation method: take the median portfolio (or the universe of investments with a similar approach and strategy) as the yardstick. Portfolio-tracking firms build databases that rank portfolios of similar characteristics and risk-return profile on a risk-adjusted return measure — letting investors see how their investments stack up against peers.

16.6

Performance attribution analysis

Attribution is the second phase of evaluation — dissecting what drove the performance so the investor can make better decisions. The return is split into two components: the part driven by the benchmark and the differential return; attribution then identifies the sources of the differential and whether it was skill or a random factor (luck).

Attribution splits total return into the benchmark-driven part and the differential return. The differential is then attributed to the allocation effect (being over/underweight sectors or asset classes) and the selection effect (picking winners / avoiding losers within the benchmark).

Tap an attribution concept

Differential return comes from allocation, selection, market timing, and (in Fama's framework) net selectivity.
Allocation effect
Being overweight a sector/asset class that outperformed the benchmark, or underweight one that underperformed. Rewards reading economy/sector trends.
Selection effect
Picking individual securities that beat the benchmark, or avoiding benchmark securities that did poorly.
Timing vs selectivity
Timing = anticipating market moves to position ahead of them; selectivity = picking extra-return securities. Most studies find market timing does not drive better performance; selectivity results are mixed.
Net selectivity (Fama)
An absolute measure: fund's annualised return minus the risk-free yield, then reduced by the standardised expected market premium × the portfolio's total risk — the excess that couldn't have come from the market portfolio.
