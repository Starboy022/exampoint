15.1–15.2

Asset allocation & correlation across asset classes

Asset allocation is the process of deciding how to distribute an investor's wealth across different asset classes — a collection of securities sharing similar characteristics and risk/return relationships (e.g. bonds, equities, cash and cash-like securities). A broad class can be split further: "bonds" into Treasury, corporate and junk bonds; equity into large-, mid- and small-cap.

Asset allocation is the starting point for an investor pursuing their goals. Professional experience shows that, over the long run, the asset-allocation decision is the major influence on portfolio performance — the choice of individual products within an asset class matters, but contributes far less to the end result.

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables, ranging from −1 to +1. +1 (−1) is a perfect positive (negative) relationship; a positive relationship means the variables move together in the same direction. Securities within the same asset class tend to be sensitive to the same economic/investment factors, so their correlation is typically high; correlation across different asset classes is typically low — which is exactly what makes diversification work. Correlations change over time and across economic regimes, so advisers should not rely solely on past correlation data.

15.3.1–15.3.3 The Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

The Investment Policy Statement (IPS) is the key output of step one — the road map that guides the entire investment process. Either the investor or their Investment Adviser drafts it, specifying objectives, goals, constraints, preferences and the risk the investor is willing to take. Every investment decision flows from the IPS, and because investor circumstances change, the IPS must be updated and revised periodically. It forms the basis for strategic asset allocation — the interaction between the investor's risk-return requirements and expected investment returns.

Its constituents typically include: the investor's goals with priorities, investment objectives with time horizon, risk/return profile, liquidity constraints, preferred (and excluded) asset classes, and a systematic review process to keep the investor on track.

15.4–15.5

Investment objectives & constraints

Investors state objectives in terms of risk–return–liquidity. Risk and return move together (higher risk, higher expected return); liquidity moves inversely with return — illiquid investments demand an illiquidity risk premium. Depending on the objective, the asset allocation tilts accordingly:

🎯 Objective → tilt

  • Capital appreciation → high-return, higher-risk assets (e.g. equity)
  • Capital preservation → safe bonds and debt securities
  • Regular income → dividend-paying stocks, interest-paying bonds, rent-paying realty

🚧 Types of constraint

  • Liquidity constraint — how much must stay accessible
  • Regulatory constraint — legal limits on investment choices
  • Tax constraint — different income types taxed differently

Regulatory constraint — the LRS example

Under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), a resident individual (including minors) can remit up to USD 2,50,000 per financial year for permissible current/capital account transactions — a hard ceiling on how much can be invested overseas. Separately, trading on unpublished price-sensitive information ("insider trading") is prohibited for those with access to it.

15.6–15.7

Exposure limits, unique needs & sustainable/ethical investing

Under SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020, the agreement between a portfolio manager and investor must include the investment approach — the broad outlay of permissible securities/instruments and the proportion of exposure. Once the investor's objective, risk appetite, liquidity, tax/regulatory constraints and horizon are known, exposure limits to specific sectors, entities and asset classes can be set to avoid concentration risk, which the manager must then adhere to.

Investors also carry idiosyncratic concerns — personal, social, ethical or cultural preferences (e.g. avoiding stocks of environmentally harmful businesses, or reluctance to sell an employer's stock out of emotional attachment). Every such preference should be recorded clearly in the IPS.

🌱 Sustainable investing

  • Considers Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) criteria alongside financial return
  • Goal: financial return and positive societal impact
  • Driven by personal values, institutional mission, or a belief that good ESG practice supports future outperformance

⚖️ Ethical investing

  • Selects investments using moral/ethical principles as the primary filter
  • Highly personalised to the investor's own views
  • Typically screens out "sin" areas — gambling, alcohol, smoking, firearms

Analysing the financial position is done through personal financial statements: a net-worth (balance sheet) statement — all assets (house, car, stocks/bonds/MF holdings, savings, jewellery, other assets) at market value, minus all liabilities (home/car/education/personal loans, credit-card dues) — and an income–expense statement, typically monthly, showing the surplus available for investment. Psychographic analysis & life-cycle analysis

Psychographic analysis bridges standard finance (which treats investors as fully rational) and behavioural finance (which treats them as normal human beings prone to biases and cognitive errors). One well-known framework is Bailard, Biehl & Kaiser (BB&K), which classifies investor personalities on two axes: confidence (confident ↔ anxious) in approaching career/wealth, and method of action (careful/analytical ↔ impetuous). Forecasting risk & return, benchmarking, and the asset allocation decision

Portfolio management integrates two sets of inputs: (1) everything learned about the investor — objectives, goals, personality type, life-cycle phase, liquidity needs, tax and other constraints — and (2) capital-market forecasts of the risk-return opportunities available. Historical risk-return by asset class is a useful starting point for forecasting, though future correlations/returns may diverge from history.

The IPS must also provide a framework for evaluating performance — a benchmark portfolio matching the composition of the investor's portfolio, so performance is compared "apple with apple". A wrongly chosen benchmark can paint a misleading picture and lead to poor decisions. Benchmarks should be reviewed periodically, and hybrid/blended benchmarks are used where the portfolio itself mixes asset classes.

The asset allocation decision

It follows logically from the investor's needs, goals, risk preferences and liquidity needs: after forecasting risk and return across asset classes, the adviser decides the asset mix that optimises the investor's after-tax returns.

Portfolio construction principles, SAA vs TAA & rebalancing

With the investor's requirements, goals and horizon established, a few principles guide the actual construction:

Strategic vs Tactical Asset Allocation

Strategic Asset Allocation (SAA) is the long-term target policy portfolio — essentially the IPS translated into asset-class weights, designed to meet the investor's goals over a long horizon. Tactical Asset Allocation (TAA) is a dynamic, short-term overlay taken within the SAA framework: when a manager believes one asset class is temporarily mispriced relative to another, they shift weights to exploit it, then rebalance back after booking gains. In short: SAA is "time in the market"; TAA is "timing the markets."

The flat navy line is the strategic (SAA) target weight. The gold line shows short-term tactical (TAA) tilts above and below it as the manager exploits — and then unwinds — perceived mispricing.
Empirical support for asset allocation's importance

Brinson, Hood & Beebower (1986) found that a portfolio's target asset allocation explained the majority of a diversified portfolio's return variability over time — confirmed by Ibbotson & Kaplan (2000): across all portfolios, asset allocation explains an average of 40% of the variation in fund returns; for a single fund, it explains as much as 90% of the variation in returns over time.

Rebalancing

Price changes in holdings drift a portfolio away from its target allocation, so it needs continuous monitoring and periodic rebalancing to restore the original risk-return characteristics (rebalancing may also be triggered by changes in the investor's goals or risk tolerance). The IPS should specify how often to rebalance and the tolerance for deviation from target.

💸 Transaction cost

  • Research and brokerage costs of buying/selling
  • Higher for relatively illiquid holdings like private equity and real estate
  • Lower/easier for liquid assets like listed equities or government bonds

🧾 Tax cost

  • Selling appreciated assets to buy depreciated ones can trigger tax liability
  • Managers set price targets for buying/selling securities
  • An opportunity cost is also possible — the sold (appreciated) asset may keep outperforming
Rebalancing is a trade-off, not a rule of thumb

If the target allocation is optimal, any drift from it is undesirable — but rebalancing itself is not free. The decision on how often and how much tolerance to allow must weigh the cost of rebalancing against the cost of not rebalancing.
