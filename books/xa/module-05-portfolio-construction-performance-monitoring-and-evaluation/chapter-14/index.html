Introduction to Modern Portfolio Theory

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) — Harry Markowitz's Nobel-winning framework from his 1952 paper "Portfolio Selection" — turned the old wisdom of "don't put all your eggs in one basket" into mathematics. It quantified diversification through covariance/correlation and established the variance (and standard deviation) of returns as the meaningful measure of risk. This chapter builds it up from first principles: the assumptions, how risk-averse, risk-neutral and risk-seeking investors behave (risk premium, fair game, certainty equivalent rate), how to compute the return and risk of a single security and a portfolio, the two-security risk formula and the power of correlation, the Efficient Frontier, the portfolio optimization process, and its estimation issues.

14.1

The framework for Modern Portfolio Theory

The idea behind diversification — "don't put all your eggs in one basket" — is ancient. Before 1950, investors were familiar with the benefits of a diversified portfolio, but they had no way of quantifying those benefits. That changed in 1952, when The Journal of Finance published Harry Markowitz's paper "Portfolio Selection." Its ideas became the foundation of Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), and Markowitz was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990.

MPT provides a framework for constructing and selecting portfolios based on the expected performance of the investments and the risk appetite of the investor. Its two mathematical breakthroughs were:

🔗 Quantifying diversification

  • Introduced the statistical notion of covariance / correlation between assets
  • Showed how much risk combining assets can remove
  • Turned "don't put all your eggs in one basket" into a formula

📐 Measuring risk

  • Demonstrated that the variance of the rate of return is a meaningful measure of portfolio risk
  • Derived the formula for the variance of a portfolio
  • Standard deviation (√variance) became the standard risk yardstick

Risk-averse, risk-neutral & risk-seeking investors

Where there is risk, more than one outcome is possible, so investors demand a risk premium — extra expected return as compensation for bearing risk. A prospect with a zero risk premium is called a fair game. How an investor treats a fair game reveals their risk attitude. Tap each type:

Three risk attitudes — tap one

Watch how each treats a fair game and how each sets the certainty equivalent rate (CER).
Risk-averse
Rejects a fair game · demands a positive risk premium (bigger for bigger risk) · makes a downward utility adjustment for risk · CER can fall below the risk-free rate
Risk-neutral
Judges only on expected return · no penalty for risk · CER equals the expected return of the risky portfolio
Risk-seeking
Willing to engage in a fair game · makes an upward utility adjustment for risk (the opposite of risk-averse)

The Certainty Equivalent Rate (CER) is the rate a risk-free investment would have to offer to be equally attractive as a given risky investment. It is how MPT converts "risky return + risk" into a single comparable number:

🛡️ Highly risk-averse

  • Assigns a risky portfolio a CER below the risk-free rate
  • Therefore rejects the risky portfolio
  • A less risk-averse investor may assign a higher CER and accept it

⚖️ Risk-neutral

  • Ignores risk entirely — no risk penalty
  • CER = expected return of the risky portfolio
  • Chooses purely on the highest expected return

Calculating the return and risk of a security

MPT needs two numbers for every asset: its expected return and its risk (variance / standard deviation).

Expected return (probability-weighted)

When future returns depend on scenarios with known probabilities, the expected return is the sum of each possible return times its probability:

E(R) = Σ ( probabilityi × returni )

Variance & standard deviation (from historical returns)

Risk is measured by how far returns scatter around their mean. Compute the mean, subtract it from each year's return to get deviations, square them, sum, then divide by n − 1. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance — a larger value means larger risk.

Variance = Σ (Ri − R̄)² ⁄ (n − 1)  ·  SD = √Variance

The expected return of a portfolio is a weighted average

For a portfolio, the expected return is the weighted average of the constituents' expected returns. Workbook example — A (weight 0.2, return 9%), B (0.1, 12%), C (0.3, 15%), D (0.4, 18%): the weighted returns 0.018 + 0.012 + 0.045 + 0.072 sum to 0.147, i.e. 14.7%. Risk, however, is not a simple weighted average — that is where correlation comes in (next section).

14.5

Risk & return of a two-security portfolio

A portfolio's expected return is a weighted average — but its variance is not. The variance of a two-security portfolio has three terms: the weighted variance of each security (with the weights squared) plus a weighted covariance term that depends on the correlation between them:

σ²P = wA²σA² + wB²σB² + 2·wA·wB·ρAB·σA·σB

The correlation ρ is the whole game. At ρ = +1 the securities move in lockstep and portfolio risk is just the weighted-average SD (no benefit). As ρ falls, the covariance term shrinks and portfolio risk drops below the weighted average — that is the mathematical face of diversification. The Efficient Frontier

Plot every feasible portfolio with risk (standard deviation) on the horizontal axis and expected return on the vertical axis, and the combinations fill a region. Its upper-left boundary is the Efficient Frontier: the set of portfolios giving the highest expected return for each level of risk (equivalently, the lowest risk for each level of return). Any portfolio below the frontier is inefficient — you can do better on return, risk, or both.

14.8

Estimation issues

MPT is only as good as the numbers fed into it. The expected returns, volatilities and correlations that drive the optimizer are estimates — usually drawn from historical data — and they carry error. Small errors in the inputs can shift the efficient frontier and the "optimal" portfolio, and past relationships may not hold in the future.

Expected returns are estimated Volatilities are estimated Correlations are estimated Often based on historical data Estimation error propagates Past ≠ future
Garbage in, garbage out

Because the optimizer's outputs (the efficient frontier and optimal portfolio) depend entirely on estimated inputs, an adviser must treat MPT results as indicative, not exact. Correlations in particular can change sharply — often rising toward 1 in a crisis, exactly when diversification is most needed.
