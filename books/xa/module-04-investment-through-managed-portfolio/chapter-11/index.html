11.1

What a mutual fund is

A mutual fund is an investment product that lets investors get exposure to an asset class of their choice — equity, debt, gold or real estate — through a pooled, professionally managed vehicle, without having to invest directly in the markets. Several regulated entities make it work. Follow the chain:

The sponsor promotes the fund; trustees safeguard investors; the AMC launches and manages schemes (with SEBI and trustee approval); the R&T agent keeps investor records and the custodian safe-keeps assets. The pooled portfolio is owned by the investors, in proportion to their units.
NFO, SID and KIM

A scheme first invites subscription through a New Fund Offer (NFO) via an offer document setting out the investment objective, asset pattern, strategy, costs and fees. The full Scheme Information Document (SID) and the abridged Key Information Memorandum (KIM) — attached to the application form — are on the AMC's website.

Why a redemption at NAV doesn't move the NAV

When an investor redeems 1,000 units of NUM Fund at ₹12, net assets fall by ₹12,000 (to ₹1,08,000) and units fall by 1,000 (to 9,000) — so NAV = 1,08,000 ÷ 9,000 = ₹12, unchanged. The decrease in net assets is exactly offset by the decrease in units. The NAV only changes if units are allotted at a price different from NAV — which is why every transaction must be at NAV.

Regulatory framework

Mutual fund products

SEBI categorises open-end products as equity, debt, hybrid, and other schemes, with generally one scheme per category per fund (exceptions: index funds/ETFs on different indices, FoFs with different underlyings, and sectoral/thematic funds).

Hybrid & other funds

Capital Protection Fund — the ₹90 → ₹100 idea

A closed-end hybrid invests, say, ₹90 in debt for 3 years so it grows to ₹100 at maturity (net of expenses), protecting the capital, while the remaining ₹10 goes into equity derivatives for upside. The debt leg guarantees the floor; the derivative leg supplies the potential gain.

11.6

Investment options

How the investor takes returns — and how they are taxed — depends on the option chosen for the scheme:

📈 Growth option

  • Returns are retained in the scheme
  • They appreciate the NAV (and the value of the holding)
  • The investor realises the gain by redeeming units, any time

💸 Income Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal (IDCW)

  • Returns are paid out as periodic dividends (formerly the "dividend" option)
  • The NAV falls to the extent of the pay-out
  • The pay-out can include part of the investor's own capital — check the breakup
  • An IDCW re-investment variant buys more units instead of paying cash

11.7

Triggers

A trigger automates a sell or transfer when a pre-set condition is reached, so the investor need not track the investment continuously. Conditions can be:

A specified return over cost A specific value A NAV level A specific date An index level

When the condition is hit, the fund acts automatically — redeeming or transferring units — letting the investor achieve their aim without constant monitoring.

Systematic transactions

Investors can automate investing, withdrawing and transferring. The headline benefit of an SIP is rupee cost averaging — a fixed sum buys more units when the price is low and fewer when high, so the average cost falls below the average price.

11.10

Direct vs regular plans

Every scheme must offer a direct plan. Both plans hold the same portfolio — the difference is the expense ratio and whether a distributor is involved.

🟢 Direct plan

  • Invest directly with the fund house / R&T agent — no distributor
  • Lower expense ratio → higher NAV & net return
  • A "do-it-yourself" route — investor transacts & monitors alone
  • Suits knowledgeable investors comfortable transacting online

🔵 Regular plan

  • Bought through a distributor who assists and services
  • Pays distribution commission → higher expense ratio
  • Suits investors who need help with the process & changes

The investment adviser must ensure the investor understands the difference — cost is only one angle. The adviser assesses the investor's knowledge, time and ability to self-manage before recommending a plan, and can demonstrate how to transact directly.
