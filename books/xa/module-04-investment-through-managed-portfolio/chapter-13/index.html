Overview of Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs)

Alternative investments are everything beyond traditional stocks, bonds and mutual funds — private, off-market, and defined by their illiquidity. They exist for sophisticated investors (institutions and HNIs) willing to take higher risk for potentially higher, better risk-adjusted returns. This chapter covers how AIFs evolved in India, the SEBI (AIF) Regulations, 2012 — privately pooled structure, corpus, minimum investment, continuing interest and listing rules — the three categories (I, II, III), the nine types of AIF (venture capital, angel, private equity, debt, infrastructure, SME, hedge, social impact and special situations funds), and the role of alternatives in a portfolio.

13.1

Introduction to alternative investments

There is no uniform global classification, so alternative investments are defined simply as investments other than traditional ones. The dividing line is liquidity: traditional investments are on-market and near-cash; alternatives are defined by their illiquidity.

📈 Traditional investments

  • Financial securities — stocks and bonds (primary & secondary markets)
  • General mutual fund units and ETFs
  • On-market, liquid — exit via the market or open-ended redemption
  • Cater to general investors wanting better-than-savings returns

🧩 Alternative investments

  • Everything beyond traditional categories
  • Essential feature: illiquidity (off-market or complex, no ready market)
  • Higher potential returns with higher risk
  • For sophisticated investors — institutions & HNIs — to improve risk-adjusted returns

Internationally, AIF investors are largely institutional — pension funds, insurance companies, endowment funds, investment banks, family offices — alongside HNIs, fund of funds and AIF managers themselves.

SEBI requirements on AIFs

The SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 define an AIF as primarily a privately pooled investment vehicle — pooled from select investors (institutions and HNIs), not the general public, for a defined investment policy. Tap the core concepts:

Investment conditions & listing

An AIF may raise funds from Indian, foreign or NRI investors, only by private placement, with no more than 1,000 investors per scheme. Category I/II AIFs may invest at most 25% of investable funds in one investee company; Category III at most 10%. Investment in associates needs approval of 75% of investors by value. Offshore investment follows RBI/SEBI conditions; un-deployed funds park in liquid assets. Units of a close-ended AIF may be listed only after final close, with a minimum tradable lot of ₹1 crore.

The accredited investor route — a ₹25 lakh ticket

SEBI's accredited investor concept is optional — some AIFs use it to offer a ₹25 lakh ticket instead of ₹1 crore. An individual qualifies with annual income ≥ ₹2 crore, OR net worth ≥ ₹7.5 crore (≥ ₹3.75 crore in financial assets), OR income ≥ ₹1 crore and net worth ≥ ₹5 crore (≥ ₹2.5 crore financial). Body corporates and non-family trusts need a net worth ≥ ₹50 crore.

Categories of AIFs

For registration and operational rules, all AIFs fall into three categories, distinguished chiefly by what they invest in, whether they get incentives, and whether they use leverage. Tap one:

Category I
Socially/economically desirable — VCF, angel, SME, social venture, infrastructure & special situation funds · often incentivised · ≤25% per investee
Category II
Not I or III · no leverage except day-to-day ops · private equity & debt funds · no special incentives · ≤25% per investee
Category III
Diverse/complex strategies · may use leverage incl. derivatives · hedge funds, short-term traders, often open-ended · ≤10% per investee

Types of AIFs

The SEBI AIF Regulations name several fund types by strategy and asset. Tap each to see its definition:

Nine AIF types — tap one

Note the stage (early vs later), the asset (equity vs debt), and the special IBC-linked types.
Venture Capital (VCF)
Unlisted securities of start-ups / early-stage ventures (new products, tech, IP, business models) · includes angel funds · Category I
Angel
Sub-category of VCF (Category I) that raises from angel investors · individual: net tangible assets ≥ ₹2 cr (excl. residence) + experience; body corporate: net worth ≥ ₹10 cr
Private Equity (PE)
Equity / equity-linked instruments of investee companies · later-stage financing of established models needing scale-up · Category II
Debt
Debt / debt securities of listed or unlisted companies · incl. mezzanine, leveraged & distressed debt · often a form of PE · Category II
Infrastructure
Securities / SPVs operating, developing or holding infrastructure projects · high illiquidity & long gestation · Category I
SME
Unlisted SMEs, or SMEs listed / to be listed on an SME exchange or segment (MSME Act, 2006) · Category I
Hedge
Diverse/complex trading strategies · diverse-risk or complex products incl. listed & unlisted derivatives · Category III
Social Impact
Social ventures/enterprises meeting social norms · charitable trusts, societies, Section 8 companies, microfinance institutions · Category I
Special Situations
Invests in special-situation assets · may act as a resolution applicant under the IBC, 2016 · Category I

Role of alternative investments in portfolio management

Portfolio management must keep evolving to meet investors' return expectations. In the 21st century, generating returns from established models has grown harder — driven by:

Technological innovation Environmental concerns Regulatory challenges Economic volatility Geo-political issues Changing customer needs

Expansionary monetary policy and zero or negative benchmark rates in developed economies pushed returns down there and drove a shift to emerging markets over the past two decades. Crucially, the rationale for alternatives is not just about returns — investors use them to improve risk-adjusted returns and for diversification beyond traditional assets.
