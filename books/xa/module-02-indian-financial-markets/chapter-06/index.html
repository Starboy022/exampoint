6.1

Nature and definition of the primary market

A company's early capital comes from its promoters and their associates. As it needs more, it turns to a wider group of "outside" investors — investors not associated with the promoters. The primary market is where equity or debt funds are raised by companies from these outside investors through an offer of securities.

In the primary market, investors buy the security directly from the issuer. It is also called the "new issue market", because the securities are issued for the first time. Expanding an issuer's ability to raise capital from public investors who were not part of the early stages is known as "going public". This makes long-term capital available from a larger number of investors.

In the primary market, capital flows to the issuer for newly created securities. In the secondary market, the same securities change hands between investors — the issuer receives nothing.

Government securities are gilt-edged

Governments raise money through G-Secs: short-term treasury bills (original maturity < 1 year — only the Central Government issues these, at 91, 182 and 364 days) and long-term dated securities/bonds (1 year or more). State Governments issue only bonds — the State Government Securities (SGS). G-Secs carry no default risk, hence "risk-free gilt-edged". REITs/InvITs and AIFs are special issuers: REITs/InvITs list units (with a higher minimum investment than a normal IPO), while an AIF can only raise money by private placement — never a public invitation.

Public issue of equity: IPO & FPO

The first public offer of shares a company ever makes is an Initial Public Offer (IPO). After that, any further issue of securities to the public is a Further Public Offer (FPO). Both can be a fresh issue, an offer for sale, or a combination.

6.1.6

Pricing a public issue: fixed price vs book building

SEBI lets the issuer decide the price. It may be a fixed price (set by the company with its lead manager, disclosed upfront so investors know it before applying) or discovered through a book-building process.

In book building, the issuer specifies a floor price or a price band (from the floor to 20% above it). Investors bid a price and quantity at or above the floor. The issuer and book-running lead manager then set the cut-off price — the price at which the issue gets fully subscribed. Everyone who bid at or above the cut-off is a successful bidder.

6.1.7–6.1.10

The issue process: regulation, ASBA, debt & rights

Primary offerings are governed by SEBI's ICDR Regulations, 2018 and the Companies Act, plus RBI rules for non-resident money. They cover eligibility (net worth, profitability track record), the process and timelines, and how the funds may be used.

ASBA — Application Supported by Blocked Amount

Applications to a public issue must use ASBA: the application money is blocked in the investor's bank account and released only on allotment. SEBI has added UPI with block facility for retail applications through intermediaries. In a book-built offer the minimum application value must sit in SEBI's prescribed range of ₹10,000–₹15,000. Investors can bid a specific price or bid "at cut-off" — accepting whatever price the process determines, which guarantees their application is accepted.

Over-subscription and allotment

If bids at the cut-off and above exceed the shares offered, the issue is over-subscribed and shares are allotted on a proportionate basis with a refund for the shortfall. If bids fall short, it is under-subscribed and all applicants at or above the cut-off get full allotment. Shares are credited to the successful applicants' demat accounts.

Debt issues & the rights offer

A public issue of debt securities (e.g. debentures) needs a lead manager, mandatory listing, a credit rating from at least one agency (all ratings disclosed if more than one), and debenture trustees (SEBI-registered banks/FIs) to protect investors — checking that any security charged is adequate. A rights offer gives existing shareholders the right to buy more shares in a fixed ratio; the tradable Rights Entitlement (RE) has its own ISIN, so a shareholder who does not want to subscribe can sell the RE on the exchange. Rights applications too are only via ASBA.

6.2

Role and function of the secondary market

The secondary market is where securities, once issued, are bought and sold between investors. Trades here bring no new capital to the issuer — funds simply change hands. Its job is to support the primary market's capital-raising by providing liquidity, price identification, information signalling and acting as a barometer of the economy.

Price discovery helps the next raise

A company with ₹10 face-value shares trading around ₹100 can price a fresh equity issue close to ₹100 — the value investors have already discovered in the market. Rising demand pushes prices up (a signal of expected good performance); more sellers than buyers pushes them down.

Circuit breakers & price bands

An abnormal index move triggers a market-wide circuit breaker — a coordinated trading halt across all equity and equity-derivative markets nationwide, set off by whichever of the BSE Sensex or Nifty 50 is breached first. The halt length depends on the size of the move and the time of day:

10%First trigger. A coordinated halt whose duration depends on when in the day it occurs. Limits are recomputed daily on the previous day's closing index.
15%Second trigger. A longer halt — the deeper the fall and the earlier it hits, the longer trading is suspended.
20%Third trigger. Trading is halted for the remainder of the day.

Separately, price bands cap individual scrips: daily bands of 2%, 5% or 10% as specified; scrips with derivatives (or in derivative indices) have no price band but may get a 10% operating range to block non-genuine prices; all remaining scrips get a 20% band.

Novation & the Core SGF

The clearing corporation becomes the counterparty to every trade — legally stepping between buyer and seller. This is novation, and it eliminates counterparty risk: the clearing corporation settles every trade even if a member defaults. Margins fund part of this risk; the Core Settlement Guarantee Fund (Core SGF) guarantees settlement in each segment (cash, equity derivatives, currency derivatives) if a clearing member fails.

6.3

Corporate actions

Beyond running its business, a company takes actions that directly affect shareholders — sharing surplus (dividend, bonus), changing capital structure (rights, buyback), and mergers, acquisitions or delisting. All are governed by the Companies Act, 2013, SEBI regulations and the listing agreement, and require notice to regulators. Eligibility is fixed on a record date (or book-closure period): only shareholders on the records that day are entitled.

Stock split — the value stays put

A stock split reduces the face value of each share in a defined ratio, multiplying the share count to match. The company's share capital is unchanged, and — crucially — the value of your holding does not change. Companies split to lower a high share price and improve liquidity.

Split vs bonus vs rights — don't confuse them

Stock split: face value falls, share count rises, capital & holding value unchanged. Bonus (e.g. 1:3): free shares from free reserves — 1 new for every 3 held; no money paid; barred if the company defaulted on debt interest/principal or a fixed deposit. Rights (e.g. 1:1, 1:2): existing shareholders get the right to buy more shares at a set price, protecting them from dilution; the tradable RE has its own ISIN. A buyback extinguishes shares (capital falls, EPS supported); a dividend can only be paid by a profit-making company out of profits — never out of share premium, revaluation reserve or capital redemption reserve.
