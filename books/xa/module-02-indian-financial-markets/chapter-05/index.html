The Indian economy

The Indian economy has evolved from being agriculture-based to one dominated by services and manufacturing, which together contribute about three-fourths of GDP. Sustained growth depends on a robust financial system that gives the economy access to resources.

5.2

The Indian financial markets

Financial markets allocate resources efficiently by channelling surplus funds from lenders/investors to borrowers/businesses. Intermediaries — banks, mutual funds, insurers — grease this process. Markets aggregate funds, provide liquidity, and spread information so participants can make informed decisions.

Two ways to slice the market

By maturity vs by newness

Two independent cuts: by maturity — money markets (short-term, < 1 year) vs capital markets (long-term debt & equity); and by newness — primary market (new securities issued) vs secondary market (existing securities traded between investors). Beyond these, commodities, forex, insurance and pension markets round out the system.

Regulators of the financial markets

Regulation exists to safeguard investors — ensuring they can make informed decisions on a fair understanding of the markets. India runs a sectoral model: a different regulator for each slice of the financial system, with the Central Government exercising oversight and the FSDC (Financial Stability and Development Council) monitoring overall stability, sector development and financial inclusion.

Government bodies and self-regulation

Above the sectoral regulators sit government ministries. The Ministry of Finance runs five departments; two are exam-critical — the Department of Economic Affairs (the nodal agency for macroeconomic policy and the capital market, incl. stock exchanges) and the Department of Financial Services (public-sector banks, insurance and pension reforms). Tap to explore the five.

Self-Regulatory Organisations (SROs)

Under SEBI (SRO) Regulations, 2004, an SRO is an organisation of intermediaries representing a segment of the securities market, recognised by SEBI — but a stock exchange is explicitly excluded. An SRO must follow SEBI's directions, act in investors' best interest, can inspect and audit its members, and must report any member violation to SEBI. SEBI also nominates directors on the SRO's board.

5.4.1

The banking system

Banking is the core of the financial structure. Banks perform financial intermediation — pooling savings from households, governments and businesses, and lending to productive activity after a credit evaluation — and provide a secure settlement system (cheques, electronic payments). Many also distribute third-party products (mutual funds, insurance, PMS). The RBI is the regulator and monetary authority; it supervises through the Board for Financial Supervision (BFS).

Payment banks — the key limits

Payment banks promote financial inclusion but are tightly boxed in: they accept demand deposits up to ₹1,00,000, can issue ATM/debit cards but not credit cards, and offer payments/remittances — but cannot lend at all.

Money market, forex & credit infrastructure

Money market

The money market trades instruments with maturities under one year. It's highly liquid and effectively a wholesale market for institutions; the RBI intervenes here to pursue its policy objectives. The main instruments:

5.4.2

The securities market under SEBI

"Securities" is defined in Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA) — it includes shares, scrips, stocks, bonds, debentures and other marketable securities; derivatives; collective-investment-scheme units; security receipts; mutual-fund units; government securities; and other instruments the Central Government notifies (e.g. electronic gold receipts, zero-coupon zero-principal instruments). It excludes unit-linked insurance policies combining life cover and investment.

The securities market has a primary market (capital raising via new issues) and a secondary market (liquidity and price discovery). A whole ecosystem of intermediaries makes trading safe and smooth — tap each to learn its job:

Insurance market under IRDAI

IRDAI regulates the insurance market, split into life and general (non-life) insurance. The life segment was opened to private players in 2001. Life products fall into three broad buckets:

General insurance covers motor, health, travel, fire and personal accident. Products reach customers through multiple channels — agency, bancassurance, direct, broking and corporate agency. Key intermediaries: brokers (sell any company's policies, paid brokerage), individual agents (IRDAI-certified; typically one life + one general + one standalone health insurer), corporate agents (e.g. banks), surveyors & loss assessors (assess general-insurance claims), and third-party administrators (process health claims).

5.4.4

Pension market under PFRDA

PFRDA regulates the pension market, centred on the National Pension System (NPS). Two forces drive the sector: a growing elderly population and a large unorganised workforce outside formal retirement cover. India's government pension has shifted from a defined-benefit model (fixed pension by rank, no employee contribution) to a defined-contribution model (employee + employer contribute; the pension depends on the accumulated fund).

Private-sector retirement is largely covered by the Employee Provident Fund (EPFO), with contributions from both sides; part of the employer's contribution funds the Employee Pension Scheme. Some firms add superannuation or privately sponsored plans. The NPS itself is a voluntary, defined-contribution scheme open to government employees and the general public, offering a menu of fund managers and investment choices.
