Life Insurance Products

A life insurance policy is really just two building blocks — a death cover and, sometimes, a survival benefit — assembled in different proportions. This chapter takes apart every element of a policy (sum assured and the IRDAI 10×/7×/5× minimums, bonus vs guaranteed vs reversionary bonus, lapse, surrender and paid-up value), shows how to size the cover using Human Life Value and the need-based approach, then walks the full product shelf — term, return-of-premium, endowment, whole life, ULIP, mortgage and riders — always testing the inherent investment return and never losing sight of the one rule that governs them all: protection first.

2.1

The elements of a life insurance product

Every life policy is defined by the benefits it provides:

⚰️ Death cover

  • The benefit is paid only on the death of the insured within a specified period.
  • If death does not occur, no benefit is paid.
  • A term policy provides death benefit only.

🎁 Survival benefit

  • The benefit is paid when the insured survives a specified period.
  • Investment-cum-insurance policies combine death cover and survival benefit.

An insurance contract is built from these elements — tap to explore, then review the key thresholds below:

IRDAI minimum sum assured (term > 10 years)

For policies with a term of more than 10 years, the minimum sum assured is 10× the annual premium if the insured is below 45, and 7× if above 45. If the term is less than 10 years, the minimum is 5× the annual premium for everyone. (A reducing-term/mortgage policy is the case where the sum assured falls over time.)

Bonus, guaranteed bonus & reversionary bonus (investment-cum-insurance only)

Bonus is an amount added to the sum assured, declared periodically as a % of SA, paid with the maturity value or on the insured event. Guaranteed bonus is paid for the first few years (say five) as a % of SA. Reversionary bonus is declared at the insurer's discretion after the guaranteed-bonus period, only on participating policies — whose premiums are higher than non-participating policies.

Lapse, surrender value & paid-up

If premiums stop within the grace period, the policy lapses and no claim is payable. But a traditional investment-cum-insurance policy in force for at least 3 years (2 years if the term is under 10 years), with full premiums paid, acquires a surrender (cash) value — a minimum of 30% of premiums paid, rising to 90% in the last two years. (Single premium: 70% in the first three years to 90% in the last two. A ULIP has a discontinuance charge of max ₹6,000, after which the balance moves to a discontinuation policy fund; its surrender value is paid at the end of the 5-year lock-in.)

The alternative to surrender is to make the policy paid-up: the sum assured is reduced in the same proportion as premiums paid ÷ premiums due. Try the Surinder example below.

Life insurance need analysis

How much cover is enough? The amount depends on the economic value attached to a human life — the Human Life Value (HLV): the present value of the expected income over the working life of the individual that is available for the dependents. There are two common methods.

2.3

Types of life insurance products

Products are classified by their benefit pattern — what is paid at death and at maturity. Tap each to compare:

The product shelf — tap a product

From pure protection to fully market-linked savings.
Term insurance
Pure risk cover — pays only if the insured dies during the term; nothing on survival. No investment component, so premiums are the lowest and cover is most affordable. Three key factors: sum assured, premium, length of coverage. Level or increasing premium; extended cover to 85/100 yrs costs much more.
Term with return of premium
A term policy that returns the premiums at the end if the insured survives. Premium is much higher; the difference is invested by the insurer. The inherent return is found with Excel RATE. Real example → 4.61% on the extra premium.
Endowment
Investment-cum-insurance, same premium each year. On survival: investment portion + accrued bonus (declared yearly, paid at end). On death: SA + bonus accrued. "Money-back" is a payout variant. Inherent return ≈ 3–5% and not guaranteed. Judge it from the insurer's official illustration.
Whole life
Investment-cum-insurance covering the entire life (or an upper age limit), fixed premium throughout. Variants: shorter premium term, return of premium. Originally used to pass wealth free of estate duty (now abolished) — today judged like any investment-cum-insurance policy.
Unit-Linked (ULIP)
The insured chooses the portfolio (debt/equity mix). Risk-cover charges are deducted; the balance is invested; a daily NAV is announced; investment risk is on the insured. Death → SA / NAV / higher / total (per structure); survival → NAV. 5-year lock-in; more transparent than endowment/whole-life; no indicated inherent return.
Mortgage (reducing) insurance
The sum assured falls over time, matching a reducing home loan. In theory cheaper than a level term plan, but in practice often not — sometimes more expensive. As loans are usually prepaid, the extra cover goes waste, so these plans are not popular.
Same ₹1 crore of protection, wildly different premiums. Pure term costs ₹9,416/yr; the endowment costs ~33× more (₹3,16,332) because most of it is a low-return (≈5%, not guaranteed) investment. The inherent return calculator below shows what that extra premium actually earns.

Case study — Mr Jeevan: Term + Mutual Fund vs a ULIP

Age 35, cover needed ₹1 crore. Option 1: term premium ₹1,100/month + invest ₹25,000/month in a mutual fund. Option 2: a Type-II ULIP at ₹26,100/month (on death: SA + fund value; on maturity: fund value). Key differences: Option 1 is fully divisible (stop/restart investing freely) with no lock-in and higher transparency; the ULIP is indivisible for 5 years, locked in 5 years, insurance cannot be dropped even later, but offers tax-free switching between its own funds and (for pre-01.02.2021 policies) a tax-free maturity when SA ≥ 10× premium. Bottom line: prefer a ULIP over term + MF only where the insurance cover is actually required.

Riders — extra cover for an extra premium

Riders — tap one

Bolt-on benefits added to a base policy for an additional premium.
Double sum assured
Provides twice the amount insured if death occurs before the term ends.
Critical illness
Pays a pre-specified sum on diagnosis of a life-threatening illness and survival for 30 days. On an accelerated sum insured basis the life cover is reduced by the payout — cheaper, and often cheaper than a standalone CI policy from a general insurer.
Accident death benefit / disability
A periodic payout for temporary disability for a limited period, or a specified sum on permanent disability due to an accident.
Waiver of premium
Triggered by a disability or loss of income that makes premium payment difficult — future premiums are waived while cover continues.
Guaranteed insurability option
Lets the insured enhance cover without further medical examination — valuable for young people expecting higher income (and possible health issues) later.
Income benefit
On the policyholder's death, the nominee receives a periodic fixed income instead of a lump sum.
Group insurance is cheaper

When an employer covers all employees under one group policy, the premium is lower than an individual policy for the same cover, and claim processing is simpler (the employer facilitates it).

Facilities available under life insurance policies

Assignment cancels nomination — with one exception

Assignment transfers the interests in a policy (usually to take a loan). It cancels an existing nomination — except when the assignment is to the insurer itself for a loan from the insurer, where the nomination is only affected to the extent of the insurer's interest. The nomination is reinstated once the policy is re-assigned back. Assignment must be recognised by the insurer via endorsement after written notice.

2.5

Insurance under the Married Women's Property Act (MWPA)

A policy bought under the MWP Act, 1874 creates a trust that shields the proceeds from the insured's creditors — even from "beneficial nominees" whom creditors could otherwise reach.

✅ What MWPA does

  • Nominees can only be the spouse, children, or both — not parents
  • Nominees acquire all rights to the claim; the insured himself can claim nothing (even survival benefits go to the nominees)
  • Protects the proceeds from the insured's creditors
  • Declaration must be made at the time of buying the policy; works for term policies bought online or offline

⚠️ The rigidity

  • Nominees cannot be changed after purchase
  • Even after divorce, the wife continues as nominee
  • The 2015 beneficial-nominee amendment reduced some of MWPA's relevance — but only MWPA protects the proceeds from creditors

2.6

Insurance from multiple companies & telescopic premiums

Life insurance premiums are telescopic: the premium does not rise in the same proportion as the sum assured — a larger sum assured costs less per ₹1,000 of cover. So it usually makes sense to buy a large cover from the same company.

₹8 crore cover — one policy or four?

Telescopic rates stop after a level, say ₹2 crore for a given insurer. To cover ₹8 crore it can be wise to buy 4 policies of ₹2 crore each from the same company — this keeps the telescopic pricing and lets the insured give up individual policies later as the need falls.

Disclose all existing policies & pending proposals

The proposal form must list all existing policies and pending proposals with other insurers, so the insurer can judge the total cover against the insured's financial standing. Any omission is treated as failure to disclose a material fact and can be grounds to repudiate a claim or cancel the policy within the first 3 years.

Criteria to evaluate life insurance products

Online vs offline term plans. Weigh the insurer's brand and longevity, whether the sought cover/tenure is actually available, the premium after health & financial evaluation (declared rates often assume super-healthy lives), rider availability, and the IRDAI claim settlement ratio (less critical if full disclosure was made).
Traditional vs unit-linked. In a traditional plan you cannot choose the investment; returns are low, and for policies issued on/after 1 Apr 2023 with premium above ₹5 lakh, maturity proceeds are taxed as "Income from other sources". In a ULIP you choose the funds and bear the investment risk (reducible, not removable, via low-risk funds).
Investment-linked vs pure term. An insurer's primary job is risk coverage, not investments. Decouple the two elements, test whether the risk cover is adequate first — if not, eliminate the product — and only then evaluate the investment return against comparable investment products.
Online vs offline plans. Online plans are often cheaper and offer instant issuance; brokers and web aggregators provide online comparisons and premium payment/issuance.

2.8

Global coverage for different life insurance products

Two questions come up often when clients travel or hold assets abroad.

🌍 Does a policy pay if death occurs abroad?

  • Yes — defined-benefit policies (life, critical illness, accidental death) pay wherever the covered risk occurs
  • It is easy to verify that the covered event happened and its circumstances, anywhere in the world

💱 Can residents buy foreign insurers' policies?

  • Yes — using the LRS limit of USD 2,50,000 per annum, in foreign currency (useful for forex-denominated goals like overseas education)
  • But claims & grievance redress are governed by the issuing country's regulations — exercise caution
  • Evaluate the investment portion only after protection needs are met; compare with similar LRS forex instruments
