Non-Life Insurance Products

Where life insurance protects an income, non-life (general) insurance protects your assets and against unexpected large expenses — a house fire, a hospital bill, a car crash, a lawsuit. This chapter takes apart the elements of a general policy (sum insured, the one-year term, deductible, restore vs recharge, no-claim bonus), walks the whole product shelf — property, health, motor, personal accident, critical illness, travel, liability, fidelity, D&O and keyman — then works through the two ideas the exam loves: the contribution clause when you hold multiple policies, and the Top-up vs Super Top-up threshold logic (with a live simulator of the workbook's ₹15-lakh claim run). It closes on global coverage: why an indemnity health policy pays only in India but a defined-benefit life or critical-illness policy pays anywhere.

3.1

The elements of a non-life insurance product

Non-life insurance provides risk cover from the loss or destruction of assets, and cover against unexpected large expenses that would otherwise drain the individual's income. Every general policy is built from a handful of standard elements — tap each to explore:

Restore vs Recharge — two health-policy benefits that refill your sum insured

Restore restores the sum insured to 100% in case of either partial or full exhaustion of the sum insured. Recharge restores the sum insured to 100% only when it gets reduced due to a claim. Both give you a "second" sum insured within the same policy year — handy when a family floater is used up by one member early in the year.

Deductible & No-Claim Bonus

A deductible is the portion of the claim met by the insured before the insurer pays. A No-Claim Bonus (NCB) is the benefit of lower premiums in later years for each claim-free year — it can also be given as an additional bonus cover. In car insurance the NCB on the own-damage premium can rise to 50% after 5 claim-free years, and it belongs to the person, not the car (see 3.2c).

3.2

Types of non-life insurance products

General insurance spans everything from your house to your reputation. Tap each product to see what it covers and its key rules:

The general-insurance shelf — tap a product

Ten product families, from property to keyman cover.
Property insurance
Protects the structure and contents of a building against natural & man-made disasters (fire, theft, etc.). Wilful destruction and normal wear & tear are not covered. Valuables like jewellery, art & antiques need an add-on or separate policy. Usually taken with a home loan, as the lender insists on it.
Health insurance
Reimburses medical expenses for the policyholder & covered family. Claims allowed for in-patient (24-hr+ stay), domiciliary and now many day-care treatments; sub-limits per head. Cashless settles directly with the hospital; family floater covers many members under one sum insured. Pre-existing illnesses excluded for a fixed period. Portability carries over no-claim & waiting-period benefits if you switch insurer (previous policy must be unbroken).
Motor insurance
Indemnifies the insured for accidents involving the vehicle anywhere in India — (i) death/bodily injury to any person, (ii) damage to third-party property. Own-damage/theft cover is optional, but third-party liability cover is compulsory — no vehicle may be used in a public place without it. NCB up to 50% after 5 claim-free years, belongs to the person not the car; own-damage premium varies by insurer, so compare online.
Personal accident insurance
Pays a defined sum if the insured sustains bodily injury solely and directly from an accident by external, violent & visible means. Covers permanent total, permanent partial and temporary total disablement. The need for permanent-total-disability cover is often over-looked, so cover is frequently inadequate — size it like a life-insurance need.
Critical illness insurance
Pays a lump sum on diagnosis of a specified disease (cancer, heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, multiple sclerosis…), usually subject to a minimum 30-day survival after diagnosis. No payment on death (unlike life). The lump sum can be used for anything — income replacement, repaying a mortgage. Available as a standalone policy or an accelerated rider on a life policy; size it like a life-insurance need.
Overseas travel insurance
Provides medical, financial & other assistance during international travel — medical help, baggage-clearance delay, accident and add-on covers. Paid as reimbursement up to the policy maximum. May be mandatory for travel to some countries. Unusually, it is an indemnity policy designed to pay abroad (see 3.6).
Liability insurance
Indemnifies damages payable under law for personal injury to third parties or damage to their property — arising under common law (negligence) or statutory law (no-fault). Common example: professional indemnity for doctors, lawyers, investment advisers. Covers legal defence & damages, but not intentional damage or criminal acts.
Fidelity insurance
Covers losses the employer suffers from an employee's forgery, fraud or dishonesty (money or goods) during the policy period. Usually taken where employees handle large sums of cash or valuables.
Directors & Officers (D&O) liability
Insures board members, management & supervisory employees against personal liability and defence costs from claims alleging a wrongful act in their duties. Driven by exposure to shareholder claims, discrimination allegations, regulatory investigations and corporate-governance / compliance failures.
Keyman insurance
A life insurance policy a company buys on a key executive, to protect itself against the loss of that person's life. The company is the beneficiary and pays the premiums. (Listed among general covers here as a business-protection product.)
The No-Claim Bonus follows the person, not the car

When you sell your car, the buyer does not inherit your NCB — you can transfer your accumulated NCB to the new car you buy. And a fresh insurer will honour your NCB when you shift at renewal. Because the own-damage premium varies by insurer, an online comparison via a broker or web aggregator can save a substantial amount.

3.3

Benefits & limitations of multiple insurance policies

Does holding several policies help? It depends on whether they are indemnity or defined-benefit policies.

🧾 Indemnity policies (e.g. health)

  • Multiple indemnity policies make little sense — the total claim can never exceed the actual expense, and cannot exceed the sum insured across all policies combined.
  • Means dealing with multiple insurers at claim time — added complications.
  • Health premium per additional lakh drops sharply as cover rises — a ₹100-lakh policy may cost only a few thousand rupees more than a ₹50-lakh one, so one large policy is usually better.

🎯 Defined-benefit policies

  • Life insurance, critical illness, accidental death / disability policies are defined-benefit.
  • All of them pay in full, irrespective of how many such policies the insured holds.
  • So multiple defined-benefit policies genuinely multiply the payout.
The contribution clause — and when it does NOT apply

Sometimes multiple health policies are unavoidable — an employer policy plus the employee's own cover. The insured chooses which policy to claim from (normally the employer policy first). If the claim is less than the sum insured of the chosen policy, the chosen insurer cannot apply the contribution clause — it pays the whole claim. Only if the claim exceeds the sum insured of the chosen policy does the insured claim the balance from another policy, and then the insurers settle by applying the contribution provisions.

The base + super top-up strategy — ₹15 lakh cover, cheaply

A common play: buy a base policy (say ₹5 lakh) plus a super top-up (say ₹10 lakh) with a ₹5-lakh deductible → total cover of ₹15 lakh at an economical premium (the super top-up premium is quite low). A single ₹15-lakh policy is only slightly more expensive — the small difference reflects the administrative simplicity of one policy. Super top-ups are ideal for employees topping up an employer cover: set the deductible equal to the employer's sum insured, and the super top-up pays only above that.

3.4

Top-up vs Super Top-up — how the threshold is applied

"Top-up" and "Super Top-up" are not legal terms. Both are health policies that pay for hospitalisation above a pre-agreed threshold (often called the deductible). The one crucial difference:

🔹 Top-up plan

  • The threshold is applied to every single claim.
  • Only a claim that on its own exceeds the threshold triggers a payout.
  • Several smaller claims can each fall below the threshold and pay nothing.

🔸 Super Top-up plan

  • The threshold is applied once, on the aggregate of all claims in the year.
  • Once total claims cross the threshold, everything above it is paid (up to the limit).
  • Far better protection against a run of smaller claims.

Two administrative catches

Keep the base plan and the top-up plan with the same insurer where possible — otherwise you must deal with both companies whenever a claim crosses the threshold. And with a super top-up, you must inform the super-top-up insurer of every claim (even one it does not pay) so it can mark the aggregate threshold used so far.

Offline vs Online policies

💻 Online

  • All insurers offer online policies on their websites; many charge lower premiums for policies bought online directly.
  • Some offer 'web only' policies not sold offline at all.
  • Web aggregators & online brokers let you compare policies and have become major distribution channels.

🏢 Offline

  • Bought through an agent/branch; useful where guidance is needed.
  • Typically no online discount.
  • Comparison across insurers is harder without an aggregator.

3.6

Global coverage for different general insurance products

Will a policy pay if the loss happens outside India? The answer hinges on whether the policy is defined-benefit or indemnity.

Defined-benefit policies (life, critical illness, accidental death) pay wherever the covered risk occurs — it is easy to verify a death, illness or accident anywhere. Indemnity policies like health insurance must ascertain the actual expense, which is hard abroad, so they normally cover India only. The exception is overseas travel insurance — an indemnity policy designed country-by-country with arrangements to verify foreign expenses.
Buying a foreign insurer's policy — the LRS route

Resident Indians can use their Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) entitlement of up to USD 2,50,000 per annum to buy general-insurance policies of foreign companies — common when a student goes overseas and buys the insurer the university recommends. But such policies, and the grievance-redressal mechanism if a claim is refused, are governed by the regulations of the issuing country — so exercise caution.

Students going overseas — Indian insurer or foreign insurer?

It depends on the country and university. Some universities (US, UK, Europe, Australia/NZ) bundle a compulsory insurance into their fees or require a designated insurer; some only lay down conditions the policy must satisfy; a few have no requirement at all — but as overseas treatment is very expensive, cover is still advisable. Most Indian insurers know the major countries'/universities' requirements and offer suitable policies with add-ons. Other things being equal, Indian policies tend to be cheaper than similar foreign ones.
