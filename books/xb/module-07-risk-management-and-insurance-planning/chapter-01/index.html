Basics of Insurance

Insurance is the oldest and simplest form of risk management: many people who face the same risk each pay a small, certain premium into a common pool, and the few who actually suffer the loss are made whole from it. This chapter builds that idea from two vivid examples — merchant ships lost at sea and a King's employees — into the machinery every adviser must know: what makes a risk insurable, the two fundamental principles (utmost good faith and insurable interest), the working concepts (indemnity vs benefit, subrogation, contribution, co-pay, deductible, cashless claims, nominees), why insurance comes before investment, the five steps of insurance planning, and the regulations that govern health cover, ULIPs and intermediaries.

1.1–1.2

Why insurance exists — risk transfer through a common pool

Insurance is a basic form of risk management that protects against the loss of the economic benefits an asset can generate. Those assets may be physical (ships, buildings, machinery) or human (an earning individual). An unforeseen event can reduce or destroy that benefit — and there is a financial consequence to it. The consequence can either be retained (borne by the person who would have enjoyed the benefit — risk retention) or transferred to another party (risk transfer). Insurance is risk transfer: the insured pays a periodic premium to the insurer, which undertakes to indemnify the financial loss.

Example 1 — the merchant ships (19th-century London)

Every month 100 merchants each send one ship to India. A lost ship costs a merchant ₹10,00,000. On average 2 ships are lost each month, so the total monthly loss is ₹20,00,000. If all 100 merchants pay ₹20,000 each into an insurance fund, it collects exactly ₹20,00,000 — enough to fully compensate the 2 who lost their ships. So each merchant converts an uncertain ₹10,00,000 loss into a certain ₹20,000 cost. Here: risk covered = ship lost at sea; sum assured = ₹10,00,000; premium = ₹20,000.

The many pay a small certain premium; the few who suffer the loss are paid from the pool. This only works when a large number of similar units share the same peril — the merchant ship calculator below shows what happens when they don't.
Example 2 — the King's employees

A king has 500 employees, each earning ₹2 lakh/year for 10 years (gross earnings ₹20,00,000 each), and each the sole earner for his family. If 2 are expected to die in the year, the income loss is ₹40,00,000. By contributing an extra ₹8,000 per employee (just 4% of the ₹2,00,000 salary) into a fund totalling ₹40,00,000, the king assures every family of financial support on death. Risk covered = loss of income due to death; sum assured = ₹20,00,000; premium = ₹8,000. This is the seed of life insurance.

To be insurable, the event (death, accident, fire) must cause a financial loss that can be quantified. The premium depends on the expected loss and the probability of the event during the contract. That is why a person whose parents have a genetic history of heart disease, cancer or diabetes may pay a much higher health premium — the probability of a claim is higher.

1.2.1

The seven requirements of an insurable risk

Not every risk can be insured. A risk must satisfy seven requirements. Tap each to see what it means:

The seven tests of an insurable risk — tap one

If a risk fails these, insurers either cannot price it or should not cover it.
Large number of exposure units
Many similar (not identical) units face the same peril, so the insurer runs a common pool where only a few claim each year. With few participants the premium becomes unviable — if only 40 of the 100 merchants join, each pays ₹50,000 instead of ₹20,000.
Insurable interest
The insured must face a monetary loss (not merely emotional) if the subject matter is lost or destroyed, and the interest must be lawful. Merchants have insurable interest in their own ship.
Accidental & unintentional
Loss must be accidental, unintentional, uncertain and outside the insured's control. The only exception is life insurance — death is certain, but its timing is uncertain, which makes it insurable.
Determinable & measurable
Loss must be definite as to cause and amount and calculable from definite evidence — death for life insurance, fire for property, medical bills for health.
No prospect of gain or profit
It must be pure risk (only loss possible), not speculative risk. Investing in the stock market can gain or lose, so it is speculative and not insurable. This links to the Principle of Indemnity — restore, don't enrich.
Chance of loss must be calculable
The insurer must estimate, with reasonable accuracy, the average frequency and average severity of future losses.
Premium economically feasible
Premiums must be affordable and far less than the value of the risk covered — otherwise retaining the risk is more sensible than transferring it.
Pure risk vs speculative risk

Insurance covers only pure risk — situations with the possibility of loss or no loss, never gain. Speculative risk (a chance of profit as well as loss, like stock investing) is uninsurable. The Principle of Indemnity flows from this: insurance restores the insured's financial position to where it was before the loss — it must never be a source of profit.

1.3

The two fundamental principles

Insurance is a unique contract that transfers risk from the insured to the insurer. Two principles underpin it.

🤝 Utmost good faith (Uberrimae Fidei)

  • The insured knows more about themselves than the insurer — an information asymmetry
  • Screening (e.g. a medical test) is imperfect — it can't reveal all ailments or family history
  • So the insurer relies on the insured to disclose all material information truthfully — family history, medical history, smoking/drinking habits, nature of profession
  • Anything that could influence the insurer's risk perception is material; when in doubt, disclose
  • Non-disclosure discovered at claim time lets the insurer reject the claim entirely — the low premium paid is wasted and the loss is not reimbursed

🎯 Insurable interest

  • The insured must be better off if the risk does not materialise, and worse off if it does
  • A business owner has insurable interest in his own inventory — but not a competitor's
  • Direct dependents and relationships of blood and marriage have insurable interest in each other
  • Distant relatives (aunts, uncles, cousins, nieces, nephews) — only with proof of financial dependence
  • A lender has interest in the borrower up to the amount outstanding; an employer in key employees
Disclose, even if it raises the premium

An insurance policy is a promise to pay on the happening of a covered risk. It is far better to fully disclose and pay a higher premium for the extra risk than to suppress facts, pay less, and have the claim rejected when the loss actually occurs. A rejected claim wastes every rupee of premium paid.

1.4

Concepts in insurance

These working concepts recur throughout every insurance product. Start with the most fundamental distinction — indemnity vs defined-benefit policies.

💊 Indemnity insurance

  • A duty to make good the actual loss incurred
  • Requires determination of actual loss before pay-out (bills)
  • Examples: Mediclaim (hospitalisation reimbursement), property insurance, car insurance

💰 Defined-benefit insurance

  • Pays a fixed, pre-estimated sum on the covered event — no need to prove exact loss
  • Used when actual loss is hard to ascertain (life, critical illness) or too small to bother measuring (daily hospital cash)
  • Examples: Life insurance, critical illness, daily hospital cash allowance

Beyond that split, five more concepts govern how claims are shared, reduced and recovered. Tap each:

Claim-side concepts — tap one

Who recovers, who shares, who bears part of the loss, and how it's paid.
Subrogation
After paying the claim, the insurer steps into the shoes of the insured to recover the loss from the at-fault third party. It speeds the insured's payout and lets the insurer recover — a policy without subrogation rights would be more expensive.
Contribution
When the same risk is covered by more than one policy, one insurer can claim a proportionate share from the other. Applies only to indemnity policies. Two ₹5-lakh flood policies, loss ₹30,000 → each insurer pays ₹15,000.
Co-Pay
A percentage of the claim the insured agrees to bear. Claim ₹2,00,000 with 15% co-pay → insured pays ₹30,000, insurer ₹1,70,000. Higher co-pay → lower premium; it makes the insured cost-sensitive.
Deductible
A fixed amount the insured pays first, after which the claim becomes admissible. Car insurance ≤1600 CC has a compulsory ₹1,000 deductible. Types: compulsory and voluntary — higher deductible → lower premium.
Cashless claim payment
The insurer pays the hospital/garage directly instead of reimbursing the insured, avoiding a cash-flow block. Relevant to indemnity (health/car). The approved network keeps changing, so it shouldn't be the main reason to pick an insurer.
Deductible vs Co-Pay — the same claim, two policies

Claim = ₹40,000. Policy 1 (deductible ₹1,000 + co-pay 10%): ₹40,000 − ₹1,000 = ₹39,000, less 10% co-pay (₹3,900) = ₹35,100 paid. Policy 2 (deductible ₹5,000, no co-pay): ₹40,000 − ₹5,000 = ₹35,000 paid. A deductible is a fixed first slice; a co-pay is a percentage of what remains. Try both in the calculator below.

1.4

Nominees, the 3-year rule and the health moratorium

Three provisions from the Insurance Act, 1938 (and IRDAI regulations) are frequently tested.

Nominee vs beneficial nominee

Ordinarily a nominee is only a trustee for the legal heirs — they receive the money but must account for it to the heirs. But the Insurance Act, 1938 was amended in February 2015 to add the "beneficial nominee" (Section 39(7)): if the nominee of a life policy is the parent, spouse, children (or spouse and children, or any of them), that nominee is beneficially entitled to the claim — not accountable to other legal heirs.

1.5

The role of insurance in personal finance

Income is at the base of every financial plan. Any event that reduces income, stops it, or imposes a large unplanned charge on it puts the plan's goals at risk. Insurance protects the income so that goals stay on track — a life policy for an early death, a critical-illness or personal-accident-disability policy for lost earning ability, a health or motor policy for large unplanned expenses, household insurance against theft.

Insurance comes before investments — the cricket analogy

A batsman's pads and abdomen guard slow him down, yet even the greatest batsman never plays without them, because one hit could end his career. Likewise, paying a premium to protect the plan from being derailed comes before any investment is made to reach the goals. Protect first, then grow.

1.6

Investing through insurance

Some policies bundle a savings/investment component with risk protection, so the premium is higher — one part buys cover, the other is invested. That can be convenient, but it must be examined carefully.

"Give me my premium back" costs a fortune

If the merchant wants his ₹20,000 premium returned when the ship comes home safely, and investments earn 12% p.a. (1% per month), he must pay a premium of ₹20,20,000 — more than the ship itself. The insurer keeps ₹20,000 for the pool and invests ₹20,00,000, which grows to ₹20,20,000 in a month and is returned. Chasing "premium back" pushes premiums up so steeply that people compromise by cutting cover — defeating the whole purpose of insurance.

The right approach: decouple the insurance and investment elements. First test whether the risk cover is adequate — if not, reject it, because the primary goal isn't met. Only then evaluate the investment return separately, and compare it with pure investment products of a similar profile. An insurer's primary job is risk coverage; investment products are best bought from entities that specialise in them.

1.7

The role of the insurance adviser — five steps

Insurance planning is an essential part of financial planning and is specific to each individual. It follows five steps:

Identify the insurance need. Spot every situation that could cause loss of income or an unexpected charge. Needs fall into income replacement (life, critical illness, accidental disability, annuities), income protection (health, motor) and asset protection (household). A young person with no dependents may need personal-accident/critical-illness cover more than life cover.
Estimate the coverage. Size the cover to the future value of what's being replaced, the period of protection and affordability. Under-insurance means retaining part of the risk; over-insurance wastes premium. Income-replacement cover is needed during the earning phase, not after retirement.
Identify the most suitable product. Match the chosen coverage to the right type of insurance product.
Optimise the premium. Share non-mandatory information (motor), choose a higher deductible, use the no-claim bonus, and take E-Insurance (electronic policies, often discounted) to lower the premium for the same cover.
Monitor the coverage. Insurance is not one-time. Review whenever life changes the demands on income — marriage, children, buying a house, a big raise. Cover may even be reduced over time as goals are met.
The cheap option to extend the term

A 30-year-old can buy a 30-year term policy (to age 60) for ₹13,800/year, or a 40-year policy (to age 70) for ₹16,200/year. The extra ₹2,400/year buys the option to keep the policy running beyond the earning years — often worth it for the flexibility.

1.8

Regulations pertaining to insurance

1.8.1 Health insurance

  • Standard terminology across all health policies (pre-existing disease, portability, co-payment, deductible are standardised).
  • Exclusions are standardised — an insurer may have fewer exclusions but never more than prescribed. Mental illness, genetic disorders and congenital disease have been removed from the exclusion list.
  • Any health policy (except group / top-up / super-top-up) can be ported. Group policies can port to individual — within the same company in the first year of leaving, and to any company from the second year.
  • Rejection of cover needs a fair, justifiable, written reason; a claim cannot be rejected if the policy runs 5 years without a break; telemedicine is covered; delayed settlement attracts interest.
  • Every general/health insurer must offer a standard individual health product mandated by IRDAI (common wordings, seamless portability, no add-ons).
  • All individual health insurance/reinsurance policies are exempt from GST w.e.f. 22 September 2025.

1.8.2 Unit Linked Insurance Products (ULIP)

  • Full disclosure of charges for each policy year, and benefit illustrations at 4% and 8% — the prospect signs the illustration with the proposal form.
  • Lock-in period 5 years; no surrender charges after 5 policy years; partial withdrawals allowed after the 5-year lock-in.
  • Limited/regular-premium ULIPs must have level premiums and a premium-paying term of at least 5 years; all except pension/annuity must carry a mortality or health cover.
  • Charges capped from the 5th year; discontinuance charges capped; a lapsed ULIP can be revived within 3 years of lapsation.

1.8.3 Intermediaries — agent vs broker vs corporate agent

An "insurance intermediary" includes brokers, reinsurance brokers, consultants, surveyors and loss assessors. The key distinction is whom they represent. Tap a parameter:

Direct Broker vs Agent vs Corporate Agent — tap a row

The broker works for the client; the agent(s) work for the insurer(s).
Who they represent
Direct broker → the client. Insurance agent → the insurance company. Corporate agent → the insurance company(ies).
Can work with…
Broker: multiple insurers. Agent: more than one insurer per line (life/general/health) but must dispassionately advise. Corporate agent (composite): max 3 life + 3 general + 3 health insurers.
How they're paid
Direct broker: can charge a fee to the client and earn commission from the insurer. Agent / corporate agent: commission from the insurer only.
Broker categories
Direct (arranges cover for clients), Reinsurance (arranges reinsurance so insurers spread risk), and Composite (both direct and reinsurance).
Bancassurance
When a bank becomes the corporate agent of an insurer, the arrangement is called bancassurance. A composite insurance agent can't act for more than one life, one general and one health (and one each mono-line) insurer.
Suitability, benefit illustration & the SEBI (IA) carve-out

To curb mis-selling, IRDAI requires agents to justify product suitability (age, income, family status, goals, existing cover) before selling; a client may skip it only with written consent. Agents must give 4%/8% benefit illustrations and sign them alongside the policyholder, stating guaranteed vs non-guaranteed benefits. Under SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013, an IRDAI-registered agent/broker advising solely on insurance products is exempt from IA regulations — but the moment they advise on non-insurance securities/investment products, IA regulations apply and they must observe risk profiling, suitability and fiduciary responsibility.
