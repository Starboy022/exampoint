Retirement Planning Basics

Retirement is unlike every other financial goal: it has the longest accumulation and distribution periods, needs the largest corpus, and no bank will lend you money for it. As life expectancy rises, the retired stretch — 20+ years with no salary — has to be funded entirely from savings built during working years. This chapter builds the whole framework: why planning must start early and its three phases; how retirement differs from goals like a house or children's education; the four-step process (expenses → income → time horizon → corpus); the two-fold bite of inflation (with a live erosion visualiser of the ₹100 → ₹574 workbook figures); the two ways to estimate retirement income — the Replacement Ratio (Rajeev) and Expense Protection (Ashish) methods, both reproduced as a live calculator; and superannuation benefits — employer payment vs an irrevocable trust, approved funds and their Section 10(13)/10(25)(iii)/36(iv)/80C tax treatment.

4.1

The need for retirement planning

Life expectancy — the number of years a person is expected to live — keeps rising with better healthcare and living conditions. Longer life, coupled with the difficulty of estimating exactly how long, is precisely what creates the need to plan: the money to fund the retired years must be built during the working years.

The workbook's illustration — 40 years to fund 20

Take X, in his 20s. Assuming a retirement age of 60, he has a total working life of 40 years. If life expectancy is 80, then X must fund 20 years of post-retirement life with no commitment to work. Those 40 earning years must pay for both his current life and those 20 retired years.

Retirement planning is not just about accumulating money — it is about living a life of one's choice after work stops, which is only possible when planning starts early. The common mistake is waiting too long and falling short of time for adequate fund accumulation. In earlier times retirement meant a period when a worker could no longer do anything (through job loss or a layoff); today the definition has changed to a period with no work involved — for travel, leisure, hobbies, family or even a financially remunerative change of employment.

The three phases of retirement planning

Phase A

Preparation stage

Provide for child education, buy a house to live in, arrange adequate life & health insurance, and recognise the impact of ageing.

Phase B

Initial / Pre-retirement stage

Physical and psychological changes set in; one gets familiar with the retirement regulations and procedures.

Phase C

Final retirement

All necessary arrangements are complete and one is in a good position to decide freely about life ahead.

Society is getting more complex — longer life expectancy, decreasing retirement benefits, multiple job changes, rising healthcare costs — all of which make retirement one of the most important and critical elements of financial planning. Whatever the stage of life, careful planning is demanded; the earlier it starts, the more one can shape the retirement income one actually wants.

4.2

How retirement differs from other financial goals

People usually plan retirement alongside other goals — children's education, buying a house or car. All are important, but retirement is fundamentally different in two ways:

🏦 No loan is available

  • Most goals can be met with a loan if assets aren't yet accumulated — banks compete to offer finance based on earnings and work profile.
  • No company gives a loan to meet retirement needs. The corpus must be self-funded.
  • Yet most people still avoid talking about it.

⏳ It gets deferred — dangerously

  • Goals like children's education have a defined, hard time horizon (e.g. college ~14–15 years from starting school) that can't be delayed by 4–5 years.
  • Retirement, by contrast, is often postponed to adjust for other goals — a loan is taken, or the retirement kitty is withdrawn.
  • This delay can be catastrophic: requirements multiply and the early-start benefits are lost.
Why raiding the retirement kitty backfires

If college funds fall short, families often either take a loan or dip into the retirement corpus — pushing the retirement goal back a few years. But retirement has the longest accumulation period, so a few years lost early costs disproportionately: the financial requirement increases manifold and can become very hard to achieve once the early compounding is missed.

4.3

The retirement planning process

The retirement goal has the longest accumulation and distribution periods and requires the largest corpus. The income needed can only be pinned down with certainty close to retirement, yet the corpus must be built from the very start of working life — with many variables that will change repeatedly over the long horizon. That demands rigour in setting the variables and periodic monitoring. The process runs in four steps:

Determine expenses in retirement. Retirement expenses differ from pre-retirement ones. Heads include housing (utilities, maintenance, taxes), living expenses (food & personal upkeep), medical care, transportation, recreation, insurance (life/health/disability) and taxes.
Determine the income requirement in retirement. Consider maintaining the standard of living, the expenses to be met, and the inflation rate.
Set the time horizon. Two periods are central — years to retirement and years in retirement (below).
Determine the retirement corpus. The corpus that will generate the required income depends on the periodic income required, the expected inflation rate, the rate of return the corpus earns, and the period of retirement.

📆 Years to retirement

  • The period from now to the year of retirement (retirement age − current age); it shrinks each year the retirement age is unchanged.
  • Determines the inflation-adjusted cost of retirement expenses — the lower the number, the smaller the inflation effect.
  • Also drives the periodic saving needed: a longer period means greater compounding and a lower allocation from savings.

🎯 Years in / during retirement

  • From the start of retirement to the end of life for which income must be secured — it cannot be defined precisely.
  • Estimated from average life expectancy for the gender, health, genetics and lifestyle.
  • Underestimating it is longevity risk — the money may run out before life does.
The accumulation stage is where saving and investing build the corpus; the distribution stage is where that corpus is drawn down to generate retirement income. The retirement goal spans both — which is why it has the longest combined horizon of any goal.

4.3.1

The impact of inflation

Inflation is a general rise in the price of goods and services over time. As prices rise, one unit of money buys less — inflation eats away the purchasing power of money. It hits retirement planning in two ways:

① Inflating the target

  • Today's expenses must be grown to their value at retirement to find the income required at the start of retirement.

② Eroding through retirement

  • Prices keep rising during the (20+ year) retirement period too, so the income needed must keep growing every year to hold purchasing power steady.

The workbook's illustration: at 6% inflation, an item costing ₹100 today would cost about ₹179 in 10 years, ₹321 in 20 years and ₹574 in 30 years. Vary the cost and rate below to watch the erosion.

Inflation-adjusted (real) return

When a corpus earns a return but inflation is eroding purchasing power, what matters is the real return:

Real return = (1 + nominal return)⁄(1 + inflation) − 1

A corpus growing at 8% while inflation runs 6% grows in real terms at only ≈1.89% — this real rate is what should be used to discount future retirement income to today.

4.4

Estimating retirement income — two methods

There are two standard ways to estimate the income a person will need in retirement. Pick a method in the calculator to reproduce the workbook's worked example, then vary the inputs.

The two methods — tap to compare

Both estimate retirement income; they start from different anchors.
Replacement Ratio Method
Assumes the standard of living just before retirement continues. Retirement income = a ratio (e.g. 80%) of pre-retirement income, then grown by inflation every year to hold purchasing power. Easier and more accurate when defining the target from income.
Expense Protection Method
Defines retirement income from the expenses in retirement. Requires keeping a detailed monthly budget; tracking expenses gives a realistic view of retirement cost. Easier for those nearing retirement.

Reductions in living expense and taxes

Some expenses fall at retirement — a housing loan ends, children's education is over, work-related costs disappear, and some long-term savings stop. Others rise — medical care, travel. And because income often drops just after retirement, one may fall into a lower tax bracket, eliminating a good chunk of tax liability. Together these reduce the retirement income actually required.

Both methods share one limitation — the further off, the fuzzier

The more years to retirement, the less accurate either estimate is. For the replacement ratio, a distant retirement makes the income estimate imprecise (though starting early lets you hit the wage-replacement goal with a lower required return). For the expense protection method, forecasting future expenses far ahead relies on speculation. Either way, the estimate must be reviewed periodically.

4.5

Superannuation benefits to employees

Superannuation is an organisational pension programme created by a company for its employees — also called a company pension plan. Funds in a superannuation account typically grow without tax implications until retirement or withdrawal. Once an employer puts a superannuation benefit in place, it becomes a liability to meet.

No statutory pension in India — except the EPS, 1995

India has no legislation providing statutory superannuation (pension) except for employees covered under the Employees' Pension Scheme, 1995. Government bodies, nationalised banks and a few others may choose to pay pension; all other establishments (public or private) must arrange annuities through LIC or another IRDAI-approved life insurer.

An employer can meet the liability through one of two arrangements:

① Payment by the employer

  • Pay a lump-sum contribution from current revenue to buy an immediate annuity from a life insurer — sometimes as a reward for a few dedicated employees.
  • Paying pensions directly out of current revenue (pay-as-you-go) is not allowed: payments would depend on the employer's profits each year and collapse if the employer goes bankrupt.

② Funding through a Superannuation Trust

  • Create an irrevocable Trust, distinct from the employer, and appoint Trustees.
  • Transfer the contributions (employer's, and employees' if contributory) to the Trust Fund, setting aside the accruing liability every year.
Why pay-as-you-go is barred

Accounting Standard 15 (ICAI) does not allow companies to provide for retirement benefits only when they fall due — all benefits must be provided for in the year of accrual. And Section 2(11) of the Insurance Act defines life-insurance business to include superannuation allowances and annuities, making it obligatory to arrange these through an IRDAI-registered life insurer. So the employer must create a fund and set aside the liability on an accrual basis each year.

Approved superannuation funds — the tax treatment

To claim tax deduction on contributions, the employer must get the fund approved by the Commissioner of Income Tax under the rules in Part B of the Fourth Schedule of the Income Tax Act. Once approved, contributions (within prescribed limits) are a deductible business expense. Conditions for approval, and the tax sections that follow, are below — tap each section:

Conditions for approval (Part B, Fourth Schedule)

Established under an irrevocable trust connected with a trade/undertaking in India, with ≥ 90% of employees employed in India; its sole purpose is providing annuities on retirement (at/after a specified age or on incapacitation) or to widows/children/dependents on death; the employer must be a contributor; and all annuities, pensions and benefits are payable only in India.

Superannuation tax sections — tap a section

Four Income Tax provisions govern an approved superannuation fund.
Section 10(13) — payments exempt
Any payment from an approved superannuation fund — on the death of a beneficiary, in lieu of / commutation of an annuity on retirement at/after a specified age or on incapacitation, refund of contributions on death, or transfer to an NPS pension scheme under 80CCD — is not included in total income.
Section 10(25)(iii) — fund income exempt
Any income received by the trustees on behalf of an approved superannuation fund is not included in computing the trust's total income — so the fund accumulates in a tax-free environment.
Section 36(iv) — employer's deduction
Any sum paid by the employer as a contribution to an approved superannuation fund (subject to prescribed limits and CBDT conditions) is allowed as a deduction in computing the employer's business income.
Section 80C — employee's deduction
Any contribution made by an employee to an approved superannuation fund is eligible for deduction in computing taxable income, subject to the ceiling prescribed by the Income Tax Act.
Trustees' responsibilities (in brief)

The Trustees of an approved Superannuation Trust must: hold periodic meetings and keep minutes; keep the company informed of decisions; collect contributions (calculated by an Actuary for a defined-benefit scheme); invest the money per the IT Rules pattern and re-invest the interest earned; arrange the pension benefits per the options chosen; deduct tax at source on annuity payments (or advise the insurer to); and prepare, audit and submit annual accounts to the IT authorities.
